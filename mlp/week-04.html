Watch first: the animated story of this weekAbout 1-2 minutes of real 3D animation with a friendly voice. Pause any time.
Read the words of the video
  1. Imagine a car. The faster it goes, the much longer it takes to stop. A simple straight line might not perfectly show this "curvy" relationship. Our goal is to build models that can bend and capture these real-world patterns, making them smarter than just a simple straight line.
  2. To bend our simple lines, we add "curvy" features like x squared. This helps models fit complex data. But too many curves make the line wiggle wildly, fitting noise too closely. We use a 'leash' called regularisation, like Ridge or Lasso, to tame these wiggles and keep our models simpler.
  3. For example, trying to fit a simple straight line to curvy car data will underfit, performing poorly. A perfectly bent line, like a parabola, works well. But a super wiggly line tries to hit every single noisy dot, leading to huge errors on new, unseen data, which is extreme overfitting.
  4. Remember this key tip for your exams: in models like Ridge, a larger alpha means stronger regularisation. But for Logistic Regression, a smaller C means stronger regularisation! Don't mix them up. So, we learned to bend lines and tame wiggles for better predictions!

The one-minute story

Think of it like this

A straight line is the simplest model. But data is often curvy: the faster a car goes, the much longer it takes to stop. To bend a straight-line model we feed it extra columns like x2x^2x2 and x3x^3x3. That is polynomial regression: still the same linear-regression machine, just with more (curved) features.

More curviness is a double-edged sword. With a too-curvy model, the line wiggles through every noisy dot and goes crazy between them. So the second half of the week is the leash: Ridge, Lasso and Elastic Net add a penalty for big weights and tame the wiggles. You will learn which knob makes the leash tighter in each scikit-learn model (and one nasty knob, C, that works backwards).

By the end of this week you can:

  • create polynomial features with PolynomialFeatures and count them;
  • diagnose underfitting and overfitting from train and test error;
  • fit Ridge, Lasso, ElasticNet and say how coefficients behave as alpha grows;
  • pick the regularisation strength with RidgeCV or GridSearchCV;
  • say, for each model, which direction of its knob means more regularisation.

Words you need

Words you need
  • Polynomial features - extra columns made from powers and products of the originals.
  • Interaction term - a product of two different features (x1x2x_1x_2x1​x2​).
  • Underfitting - the model is too simple: poor on both train and test.
  • Overfitting - the model is too flexible: great on train, poor on test.
  • Regularisation - discouraging large weights so the model stays simple.
  • alpha - penalty strength in Ridge, Lasso, ElasticNet, SGD*.
  • Learning curve - error as a function of the amount of training data.

Idea 1: PolynomialFeatures

from sklearn.preprocessing import PolynomialFeatures
PolynomialFeatures(degree=2).fit_transform([[2, 3]])

For the single row (x1,x2)=(2,3)(x_1,x_2)=(2,3)(x1​,x2​)=(2,3) the output is

degree 2 : [1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9]          -> 1, x1, x2, x1^2, x1*x2, x2^2   (6 columns)
degree 3 : [1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 8, 12, 18, 27]   (10 columns)
degree 2, include_bias=False   : [2, 3, 4, 6, 9]     (drops the column of 1s)
degree 2, interaction_only=True, include_bias=False : [2, 3, 6]   (no pure powers)

How many output columns for nnn input features and degree ddd (with the bias column)? (n+dd)\binom{n+d}{d}(dn+d​). For n=2,d=2n=2,d=2n=2,d=2: (42)=6\binom42=6(24​)=6 ✓. For n=3,d=2n=3,d=2n=3,d=2: (52)=10\binom52=\mathbf{10}(25​)=10 ✓. The count explodes quickly as nnn and ddd grow, which is why high degrees overfit and cost a lot.

Rules of thumb:

  • The default is include_bias=True: it adds a column of 1s. LinearRegression already has its own intercept, so use include_bias=False there to avoid redundancy.
  • Scale after expanding: x10x^{10}x10 can be astronomically bigger than xxx.
  • Use it inside a pipeline: make_pipeline(PolynomialFeatures(2, include_bias=False), StandardScaler(), LinearRegression()).

Idea 2: Underfitting and overfitting, with numbers

We made 30 noisy points around the curve y=0.5x2+x+2y=0.5x^2+x+2y=0.5x2+x+2 (the true shape is a parabola), split 70/30, and fitted polynomials of degree 1, 2 and 12:

rng = np.random.RandomState(1)
xs = np.sort(6 * rng.rand(30, 1) - 3, axis=0)
ys = 0.5 * xs[:, 0] ** 2 + xs[:, 0] + 2 + rng.randn(30)
x_tr, x_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(xs, ys, test_size=0.3, random_state=0)

for d in (1, 2, 12):
    m = make_pipeline(PolynomialFeatures(d, include_bias=False),
                      StandardScaler(), LinearRegression()).fit(x_tr, y_tr)
    print(d, mean_squared_error(y_tr, m.predict(x_tr)),
             mean_squared_error(y_te, m.predict(x_te)))

1 (a line)2.143.76underfit: poor on both (high bias)

Think of it like this

Degree 12 has so much freedom it threads through every training dot, noise and all, and then swings wildly between dots. Its test error is more than 300 times the sensible model's.

Reading learning curves. Plot train and validation error against the number of training examples:

  • High bias (underfit): both curves are high and close together; more data will not help, you need a richer model.
  • High variance (overfit): a large gap: train low, validation high; more data, or a simpler/regularised model, helps.

Idea 3: Ridge, Lasso and Elastic Net

from sklearn.linear_model import Ridge, Lasso, ElasticNet
Ridge(alpha=1.0)                       # default alpha = 1.0
Lasso(alpha=1.0)
ElasticNet(alpha=1.0, l1_ratio=0.5)    # l1_ratio=1 is pure Lasso, 0 is pure Ridge
  • Ridge (ℓ2\ell_2ℓ2​): minimises ∥y−Xw∥2+α∥w∥22\|\mathbf y-X\mathbf w\|^2+\alpha\|\mathbf w\|_2^2∥y−Xw∥2+α∥w∥22​. It shrinks all weights smoothly and keeps them non-zero. It has a closed-form solution (XTX+αI)−1XTy(X^TX+\alpha I)^{-1}X^T\mathbf y(XTX+αI)−1XTy (we checked: Ridge(alpha=1, fit_intercept=False) matches the formula).
  • Lasso (ℓ1\ell_1ℓ1​): minimises 12n∥y−Xw∥2+α∥w∥1\frac1{2n}\|\mathbf y-X\mathbf w\|^2+\alpha\|\mathbf w\|_12n1​∥y−Xw∥2+α∥w∥1​ (note scikit-learn's 12n\frac1{2n}2n1​ scaling). It can set weights exactly to zero: automatic feature selection.
  • Elastic Net: a mix controlled by l1_ratio.
  • Scale your features first: the penalty treats all weights alike.
  • The intercept is not penalised.

A real run on a synthetic data set (60 samples, 8 features, only 3 informative, standardised):

LinearRegression  [ 65.6  -1.2  92.1  -1.4   1.0  91.6   0.6   2.6]
Ridge alpha=1     [ 64.8  -1.0  90.5  -1.8   0.9  89.9   0.8   2.9]
Ridge alpha=100   [ 27.1   0.8  34.3  -5.9   0.5  33.8   2.3   4.7]
Lasso alpha=1     [ 64.6  -0.6  91.4  -0.4   0.2  90.8   0.0   1.6]    (1 weight is exactly 0)
Lasso alpha=10    [ 56.3  -0.0  82.7  -0.0   0.0  82.3   0.0   0.0]    (5 weights are exactly 0)

The three big weights are the informative features. As alpha rises, ridge shrinks every weight, including the big ones (27 vs 65 at alpha 100), but none hits zero; Lasso keeps the big ones and zeroes the junk (five zeros at alpha 10).

Which knob tightens the leash? (the exam asks: "which model has the lowest overfitting, i.e. the largest regularisation?")

ModelKnobMore regularisation means
Ridge, Lasso, ElasticNetalphalarger alpha
SGDRegressor / SGDClassifieralphalarger alpha
LogisticRegressionC (inverse strength)smaller C
SVC, LinearSVC, SVRCsmaller C
DecisionTree*max_depth, min_samples_leafsmaller depth, larger leaf size
KNeighbors*n_neighborslarger kkk
MLP*alpha (L2)larger alpha
Watch out - real exam trap

C works backwards. In LogisticRegression and the SVM classes, C is the inverse of the regularisation strength: small C === strong regularisation, large C === weak. In Ridge/Lasso/SGD*, large alpha === strong regularisation. Do not mix them up.

Idea 4: Choosing alpha

from sklearn.linear_model import RidgeCV
RidgeCV(alphas=[0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100], cv=5).fit(X, y)       # picks the best alpha by CV
from sklearn.model_selection import GridSearchCV
GridSearchCV(Ridge(), {"alpha": [0.01, 0.1, 1, 10]}, cv=5).fit(X, y)
GridSearchCV(pipe, {"model__alpha": [0.01, 0.1, 1, 10]}, cv=5)       # inside a pipeline

All three are correct methods to search for the best regularisation parameter; the grid key inside a pipeline needs the step name with a double underscore. There are also LassoCV and ElasticNetCV. Regularisation can also come from early stopping: SGDRegressor(early_stopping=True) holds out validation_fraction=0.1 of the data and stops when the validation score stops improving.

Solved examples

Solved example: 1. How many polynomial features?

Question. Three features, degree 3, with the bias column. How many output columns? And without bias?

Solution. (3+33)=(63)=20\binom{3+3}{3}=\binom63=\mathbf{20}(33+3​)=(36​)=20 with the bias, so 19\mathbf{19}19 without (include_bias=False).

Solved example: 2. Which has the least overfitting?

Question. Which model is the most regularised? (a) Ridge(alpha=0.01) (b) Ridge(alpha=100) (c) LogisticRegression(C=100) (d) LogisticRegression(C=0.01).

Solution. Compare within each family. For Ridge, bigger alpha is stronger, so (b) beats (a). For logistic regression, C is inverse, so C=0.01C=0.01C=0.01 (d) is stronger than C=100C=100C=100 (c). The two strongly regularised settings are (b) and (d); (a) and (c) are the weak ones. (You cannot rank a Ridge alpha against a logistic C by the numbers alone, so an exam option list normally keeps one family at a time.)

Solved example: 3. Reading the coefficients

Question. A Lasso fit gives coef_ = [3.2, 0.0, -1.5, 0.0]. What does it say?

Solution. Features 2 and 4 have been eliminated (their weights are exactly zero), so Lasso judged them unhelpful. Features 1 and 3 matter: feature 1 raises the prediction (+3.2 per unit), feature 3 lowers it (-1.5 per unit). Ridge would never give an exact zero.

Show the full solution

Watch out - real exam trap
  • C is inverse regularisation (logistic, SVM); alpha is direct (ridge, lasso, SGD).
  • PolynomialFeatures adds a bias column by default; use include_bias=False with LinearRegression.
  • Lasso can zero weights; Ridge cannot.
  • Scale features before regularising.
  • Increasing the polynomial degree always lowers training error, never promising lower test error.

Recap

Remember this
  • PolynomialFeatures(degree): (n+dd)\binom{n+d}{d}(dn+d​) columns (with bias); include_bias=False, interaction_only=True. Scale afterwards.
  • Underfit: high error on both; overfit: low train, high test. Learning curves show the gap.
  • Ridge shrinks, Lasso zeroes (and uses 12n\frac1{2n}2n1​), ElasticNet mixes (l1_ratio). alpha ↑\uparrow↑ = more regularisation, but C ↓\downarrow↓ = more regularisation.
  • Tune with RidgeCV, GridSearchCV (step__alpha in a pipeline).
Formulas to keep
  • Polynomial feature count: (n+dd)\binom{n+d}{d}(dn+d​) (with bias), minus 1 without
  • Ridge: ∥y−Xw∥2+α∥w∥2\|\mathbf y-X\mathbf w\|^2+\alpha\|\mathbf w\|^2∥y−Xw∥2+α∥w∥2; w=(XTX+αI)−1XTy\mathbf w=(X^TX+\alpha I)^{-1}X^T\mathbf yw=(XTX+αI)−1XTy
  • Lasso (sklearn): 12n∥y−Xw∥2+α∥w∥1\frac1{2n}\|\mathbf y-X\mathbf w\|^2+\alpha\|\mathbf w\|_12n1​∥y−Xw∥2+α∥w∥1​
  • Defaults: alpha=1.0 (Ridge, Lasso, ElasticNet), l1_ratio=0.5 (ElasticNet)
  • More regularisation: alpha ↑\uparrow↑; C ↓\downarrow↓
