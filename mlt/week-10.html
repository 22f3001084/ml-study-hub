Read the words of the video
  1. Imagine kids on a playground, boys on one side, girls on the other. You need to draw a line to separate them. Many lines could work! But which line is the very best? Think of this line as the middle of a road. We want the widest possible road that still keeps all the kids safely on their own sides. This idea is called a Support Vector Machine, aiming for the 'maximum margin'.
  2. This special road has two clear edges. Only the few kids standing right on these edges truly matter for defining the road! These important 'edge-standers' are called 'support vectors.' If you moved any other kid, the road would not change. The best separating line is found by making this road as wide as possible, or 'maximizing the margin'.
  3. Let's try a small example. Imagine two points on a number line: one at zero, labelled negative; another at two, labelled positive. The best separating line will be exactly in the middle, at one. The 'road' would have a width of two, with its edges at zero and two. Both original points become our support vectors, defining this perfect road.
  4. SVMs are super important for your exams! Remember, the margin is two divided by the weight vector's length. Support vectors are the points on the road's edge. A big 'C' means a narrow road, while a small 'C' means a wide road. And with cool tricks, SVMs can even find curved roads! Keep practicing, you've got this!

The one-minute story

Think of it like this

Two groups of kids stand on a playground, boys on the left, girls on the right, and you want to draw a line between them. Many lines work. Which one is best?

Think of the line as the centre of a road. A thin road might be squeezed against some child on either side; any wobble and that child is on the wrong side. The safest line is the one at the centre of the widest possible road that still has nobody standing on it. That is the support vector machine (SVM): maximum margin.

Here is the beautiful surprise. Only the kids standing right at the edges of the road matter. If you moved any child further back, or removed them, the road would not change. Those edge-standers are the support vectors, and they alone decide where the road goes. And thanks to the kernel trick from week 2, the same machine can draw curved roads.

By the end of this week you can:

  • state the hard-margin SVM problem and compute the margin;
  • explain the dual problem and the role of support vectors (via complementary slackness);
  • extend to the soft margin with slack variables and the parameter CCC;
  • use the kernel trick to get non-linear boundaries and read hinge loss.

Words you need

Words you need
  • Margin - the width of the empty road between the two classes. A wider margin is more robust.
  • Support vectors - the training points that lie exactly on the edge of the road (or inside it, in the soft-margin case).
  • Hard margin - insist that nobody is inside the road: needs separable data.
  • Soft margin - allow a few points inside or across, at a price.
  • Slack variable ξi≥0\xi_i\ge0ξi​≥0 - how far point iii violates the road.
  • CCC - the price of violations. Large CCC: strict. Small CCC: relaxed.
  • Hinge loss - max⁡(0, 1−y f(x))\max(0,\,1-y\,f(\mathbf x))max(0,1−yf(x)).

Idea 1: Maximising the margin

Labels yi∈{−1,+1}y_i\in\{-1,+1\}yi​∈{−1,+1} and a linear classifier f(x)=wTx+bf(\mathbf x)=\mathbf w^T\mathbf x+bf(x)=wTx+b. We scale w,b\mathbf w,bw,b so that the closest points of each class satisfy ∣f(x)∣=1|f(\mathbf x)|=1∣f(x)∣=1. Then:

  • every training point obeys yi(wTxi+b)≥1y_i(\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_i+b)\ge1yi​(wTxi​+b)≥1 (correct side, outside the road);
  • the two road edges are wTx+b=±1\mathbf w^T\mathbf x+b=\pm1wTx+b=±1, and the width of the road is
margin=2∥w∥\text{margin}=\frac2{\|\mathbf w\|}margin=∥w∥2​

Making the road as wide as possible means making ∥w∥\|\mathbf w\|∥w∥ as small as possible:

The idea

Hard-margin SVM (primal)

min⁡w,b 12∥w∥2subject toyi(wTxi+b)≥1  for all i\min_{\mathbf w,b}\ \frac12\|\mathbf w\|^2\qquad\text{subject to}\qquad y_i(\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_i+b)\ge1\ \text{ for all }iw,bmin​ 21​∥w∥2subject toyi​(wTxi​+b)≥1  for all i

This is a convex problem (quadratic objective, linear constraints) with a unique solution.

Question. One point x=0x=0x=0 with y=−1y=-1y=−1 and one point x=2x=2x=2 with y=+1y=+1y=+1. Find the maximum-margin classifier and its margin.

Solution. In 1-D, f(x)=wx+bf(x)=wx+bf(x)=wx+b. We need −(w⋅0+b)≥1-(w\cdot0+b)\ge1−(w⋅0+b)≥1, i.e. b≤−1b\le-1b≤−1, and w⋅2+b≥1w\cdot2+b\ge1w⋅2+b≥1. Idea 3: The soft margin

Real data is rarely perfectly separable, or it is separable only by a skinny, fragile road. Allow violations with slack ξi≥0\xi_i\ge0ξi​≥0:

The idea

Soft-margin SVM

min⁡w,b,ξ 12∥w∥2+C∑iξis.t.yi(wTxi+b)≥1−ξi,  ξi≥0\min_{\mathbf w,b,\boldsymbol\xi}\ \frac12\|\mathbf w\|^2+C\sum_i\xi_i\quad\text{s.t.}\quad y_i(\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_i+b)\ge1-\xi_i,\ \ \xi_i\ge0w,b,ξmin​ 21​∥w∥2+Ci∑​ξi​s.t.yi​(wTxi​+b)≥1−ξi​,  ξi​≥0
  • ξi=0\xi_i=0ξi​=0: the point is correctly outside the road. 0<ξi<10<\xi_i<10<ξi​<1: inside the road but on the right side. ξi>1\xi_i>1ξi​>1: on the wrong side (misclassified).
  • CCC is the price of a violation. Large CCC: violations are expensive, so the margin is narrow and few points violate it (low bias, risk of overfitting). Small CCC: violations are cheap, so the margin is wide and many points may violate (more bias, underfitting risk).
  • In the dual the only change is the box constraint 0≤αi≤C0\le\alpha_i\le C0≤αi​≤C. Support vectors are the points with αi>0\alpha_i>0αi​>0: on the edge, inside the road, or misclassified.

Hinge loss view. Eliminating ξi\xi_iξi​ gives the unconstrained problem

min⁡ 12∥w∥2+C∑imax⁡(0,  1−yi(wTxi+b))\min\ \frac12\|\mathbf w\|^2+C\sum_i\max\big(0,\;1-y_i(\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_i+b)\big)min 21​∥w∥2+Ci∑​max(0,1−yi​(wTxi​+b))

The hinge loss is 000 for points beyond the road edge and grows linearly as a point enters the road or crosses over. Compared with logistic loss (week 9), hinge loss goes exactly to zero for "safe" points, which is why those points drop out.

Idea 4: Non-linear SVMs

Use the kernel trick: the dual and the prediction only need k(xi,xj)k(\mathbf x_i,\mathbf x_j)k(xi​,xj​). With an RBF kernel the boundary can wrap around clusters. Choices: kernel type (linear, polynomial, RBF) and its parameters (degree, σ\sigmaσ), plus CCC, all chosen by cross-validation. A very small σ\sigmaσ (very local RBF) lets the SVM memorise: overfits.

Watch out - real exam trap
  • Margin is 2∥w∥\frac{2}{\|\mathbf w\|}∥w∥2​, not 1∥w∥\frac1{\|\mathbf w\|}∥w∥1​ (that is the distance from the boundary to one edge).
  • Large CCC ⇒\Rightarrow⇒ narrow margin, small C⇒C\RightarrowC⇒ wide margin. Easy to flip.
  • Support vectors have αi>0\alpha_i>0αi​>0; in the hard-margin case they lie exactly on yif=1y_if=1yi​f=1.
  • Hard margin needs linearly separable data; soft margin does not.
  • Don't forget the dual constraint ∑iαiyi=0\sum_i\alpha_iy_i=0∑i​αi​yi​=0.

Quick quiz

Quick quiz

The margin of a hard-margin SVM with weight vector w is...

  • ||w||
  • 1 / ||w||
  • 2 / ||w||
  • ||w|| squared
The two edges are at w.x + b = +1 and -1, so the gap is 2 / ||w||.

A support vector is a training point...

  • far from the boundary
  • on (or inside) the margin, with a non-zero dual weight
  • that is misclassified only
  • at the origin
Complementary slackness gives non-zero alpha only for points on the margin edge (or violating it in the soft case).

In the soft-margin SVM, increasing C...

  • widens the margin
  • penalises margin violations more, narrowing the margin
  • removes the slack variables
  • has no effect
A big C makes violations expensive.
