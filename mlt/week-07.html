Read the words of the video
  1. Imagine a new, strange fruit arrives, and you don't know what it is. You check the three fruits in your basket that look most similar in size and color. If two are mangoes and one is a peach, you might decide your new fruit is a mango. This is exactly how k-Nearest Neighbours works!
  2. Machine learning models help us classify things. One idea, k-Nearest Neighbours, decides by looking at its closest friends and taking a majority vote. Another, Decision Trees, plays a game of 'twenty questions', asking smart questions to sort things into clear groups. Both are powerful tools.
  3. Let's try k-NN. Imagine points on a map. A new point arrives, and we want to classify it. If we look at just one neighbour, its class might be positive. But looking at three neighbours, it's still positive. Yet, with five neighbours, the answer actually changes to negative!
  4. For exams, remember: k-NN needs features scaled, but decision trees don't. A 1-nearest neighbour model usually has zero training error, but it likely overfits, meaning it's memorized the data too much. Pruning helps trees avoid this. Always aim for a balanced model!

The one-minute story

Think of it like this

k-NN: "tell me who your neighbours are". A new fruit arrives and you have no idea what it is. So you look at the 3 fruits in your basket that look most like it (size, colour, weight). Two are mangoes and one is a peach: you call it a mango. No rules were learned in advance; you just remember the examples and look things up when asked.

Decision tree: "twenty questions". To guess an animal, you ask the question that splits the possibilities best ("Does it live in water?"). Then, depending on the answer, you ask the next best question for that group. A decision tree is that game written down as a flowchart, built automatically from data by always choosing the question that makes the groups cleanest.

Two very different ideas, both for classification (and they also do regression), both easy to read, and both at risk of overfitting.

By the end of this week you can:

  • run k-NN by hand and see how kkk changes the answer;
  • explain the curse of dimensionality and the need for feature scaling;
  • compute entropy, Gini impurity and information gain;
  • build the first split of a decision tree and explain overfitting and pruning.

Words you need

Words you need
  • Classification - predicting a category (a class label).
  • Nearest neighbour - the training point with the smallest distance to the query.
  • Lazy learner - a method that does no training; it stores the data and works at prediction time (k-NN).
  • Entropy HHH - a measure of how mixed (unpredictable) a group is: 0 for a pure group, largest for an even mix.
  • Gini impurity - another measure of mixedness.
  • Information gain - how much a split reduces entropy.
  • Pruning - cutting a tree back so it does not memorise the training data.

Idea 1: k-nearest neighbours

The idea

k-NN classification. To classify a new point q\mathbf qq:

  1. Compute the distance from q\mathbf qq to every training point (usually Euclidean).
  2. Take the kkk closest.
  3. Predict the majority class among them.

k-NN regression: predict the average of those kkk targets.

Same data, same query: the answer changes with kkk. (For kkk-NN regression with neighbour targets 3, 5, 103,\,5,\,103,5,10 the prediction is their mean, 666.)

Choosing kkk and other facts

  • k=1k=1k=1: the boundary follows every point, zero training error, very jagged: overfits (high variance). Its training "error" is meaningless because each point is its own neighbour.
  • k=nk=nk=n: always predicts the overall majority class: underfits (high bias).
  • Pick kkk by cross-validation. Use an odd kkk for two classes to avoid ties.
  • Scale your features. Distance is dominated by features with big numbers (salary in rupees vs age in years). Standardise first.
  • Curse of dimensionality. In very many dimensions, all points look about equally far away, so "nearest" stops meaning anything and you need exponentially more data.
  • No training, slow prediction: every query scans the whole data set.
  • Weighted k-NN gives closer neighbours more say.

Idea 2: Decision trees

A decision tree asks a question about one feature at each node (such as "age <30<30<30?"), sends the example down the matching branch, and ends at a leaf that holds the prediction (the majority class of the training examples that reached it).

How is the question chosen? Greedily: among all possible splits, pick the one that makes the two child groups purest. We need a number for "purity".

Entropy

For a group in which a fraction pcp_cpc​ belongs to class ccc:

H=−∑cpclog⁡2pcH=-\sum_cp_c\log_2p_cH=−c∑​pc​log2​pc​
  • A pure group (p=1p=1p=1): H=0H=0H=0.
  • Two classes split 50/50: H=1H=1H=1 bit, the maximum.
  • 75/25: H=−0.75log⁡20.75−0.25log⁡20.25=0.311+0.5=0.811H=-0.75\log_20.75-0.25\log_20.25=0.311+0.5=0.811H=−0.75log2​0.75−0.25log2​0.25=0.311+0.5=0.811.

Gini impurity

G=1−∑cpc2G=1-\sum_cp_c^2G=1−c∑​pc2​

Pure: 000. 50/50: 0.50.50.5. 75/25: 1−(0.5625+0.0625)=0.3751-(0.5625+0.0625)=0.3751−(0.5625+0.0625)=0.375. (A cheaper, very similar measure.)

Information gain

Splitting a group into children, the information gain is the parent's entropy minus the size-weighted average entropy of the children:

IG=H(parent)−∑children jnjnH(child j)IG=H(\text{parent})-\sum_{\text{children }j}\frac{n_j}{n}H(\text{child }j)IG=H(parent)−children j∑​nnj​​H(child j)

Pick the split with the largest information gain.

Solved example: 2. Which split is better?

Question. A node holds 8 examples: 4 positive, 4 negative. Split A gives children (3+,1−)(3+,1-)(3+,1−) and (1+,3−)(1+,3-)(1+,3−). Split B gives (4+,0−)(4+,0-)(4+,0−) and (0+,4−)(0+,4-)(0+,4−). Compute the information gain of each.

Solution. Parent: H=1H=1H=1.

  • Split A: each child is 75/25, entropy 0.8110.8110.811. Weighted average =48(0.811)+48(0.811)=0.811=\frac48(0.811)+\frac48(0.811)=0.811=84​(0.811)+84​(0.811)=0.811. IG=1−0.811=0.189IG=1-0.811=\mathbf{0.189}IG=1−0.811=0.189.
  • Split B: both children are pure, entropy 0. IG=1−0=1IG=1-0=\mathbf1IG=1−0=1.

Split B is perfect, so choose B. (With Gini: parent 0.50.50.5; A: weighted 0.3750.3750.375, gain 0.1250.1250.125; B: gain 0.50.50.5. The same winner.)

Growing and pruning the tree

Repeat the process on each child until a stopping rule applies: the node is pure, or no features are left, or the depth limit or minimum node size is reached.

  • A tree grown until every leaf is pure gets zero training error but usually overfits: it memorises noise.
  • Control complexity with a maximum depth, a minimum number of examples per leaf, or pruning (grow big, then cut back branches that do not help on validation data).
  • Regression trees split to reduce variance (squared error) and predict the leaf's mean.

Pros: easy to read, no feature scaling needed, handles mixed feature types. Cons: unstable (small data changes can change the tree), axis-aligned boundaries only, and overfits when deep. Both problems are repaired by ensembles (week 11).

Watch out - real exam trap
  • Information gain uses the weighted average of child entropies (weights are group sizes).
  • log⁡2\log_2log2​, not ln⁡\lnln, for entropy in bits (check what the question uses).
  • 1-NN training error is zero; do not interpret that as a good model.
  • Scale features for k-NN; not for trees.
  • A fully grown tree is not a good sign: it probably overfits.

Recap

Remember this
  • k-NN: majority vote (or mean) of the kkk nearest points. Small kkk overfits, large kkk underfits. Scale features; beware high dimensions.
  • Entropy −∑plog⁡2p-\sum p\log_2p−∑plog2​p; Gini 1−∑p21-\sum p^21−∑p2; information gain =Hparent−∑njnHj=H_{\text{parent}}-\sum\frac{n_j}nH_j=Hparent​−∑nnj​​Hj​.
  • Tree: greedily pick the split with the biggest gain; grow, then prune or limit depth to avoid overfitting.
