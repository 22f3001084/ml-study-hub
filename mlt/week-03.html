Read the words of the video
  1. Imagine a city needing three new pizza shops. We don't know the neighborhoods, only where houses are. We drop three shops, then each house goes to its nearest shop. Next, each shop moves to the center of its customer crowd. We repeat this, moving shops and re-assigning customers, until nothing changes.
  2. This game is called k-means clustering. Its goal is to group data points without labels. We want to make sure every point is as close as possible to its group's center. Lloyd's algorithm does this: first, we assign each point to its nearest center, then we update each center to be the mean of its assigned points, and repeat until stable.
  3. Let's try a simple example with points on a line: one, two, three, ten, eleven, twelve. We start with two centers. Points one, two, three initially group with the first center, and the rest with the second. We update the first center to be the average of one, two, and three, making it two. The second center becomes the average of its points, eleven. Then, points are reassigned.
  4. Remember, k-means always stops in finite steps, reaching a local minimum, not necessarily the best overall. It doesn't find the global optimum. K-means++ helps by picking spread-out starting centers using squared distance. You cannot choose 'k' just by minimizing the objective; use the elbow method. Keep practicing the steps!

The one-minute story

Think of it like this

A city wants to open 3 pizza shops so that everybody has a shop nearby. Nobody has told you the neighbourhoods; you just have a map of where houses are.

Try this game:

  1. Drop 3 shops anywhere on the map.
  2. Every house walks to its nearest shop. Now each shop has a crowd of customers.
  3. Move each shop to the centre of its own crowd (the average of the houses that chose it).
  4. Houses may now be closer to a different shop. Repeat steps 2 and 3.

After a few rounds nothing changes any more: every shop sits in the middle of its crowd and every house is at its nearest shop. You have just clustered the houses into 3 groups without anyone labelling them. That is k-means.

By the end of this week you can:

  • state the k-means objective and run Lloyd's algorithm by hand;
  • prove (in words) that it always converges, and say what it converges to;
  • seed it well with k-means++;
  • explain how kernel k-means handles non-round clusters.

Words you need

Words you need
  • Cluster - a group of points that are similar to each other.
  • Centroid (or centre, mean) μk\boldsymbol\mu_kμk​ - the average of the points in cluster kkk.
  • kkk - the number of clusters, chosen by you before you start.
  • Objective FFF - the total squared distance from every point to its own centroid. Smaller is a tighter clustering.
  • Lloyd's algorithm - the "assign, then update" loop.
  • Local optimum - a result that cannot be improved by small changes, but might not be the best overall.

Idea 1: What is k-means trying to do?

You have points x1,…,xn\mathbf x_1,\dots,\mathbf x_nx1​,…,xn​ and want to split them into kkk groups. Let zi∈{1,…,k}z_i\in\{1,\dots,k\}zi​∈{1,…,k} record which group point iii belongs to, and μ1,…,μk\boldsymbol\mu_1,\dots,\boldsymbol\mu_kμ1​,…,μk​ be the group centres. The k-means objective is

F(z,μ)=∑i=1n∥xi−μzi∥2F(z,\boldsymbol\mu)=\sum_{i=1}^n\big\|\mathbf x_i-\boldsymbol\mu_{z_i}\big\|^2F(z,μ)=i=1∑n​​xi​−μzi​​​2

the sum of squared distances from each point to its own centre. Finding the global minimum over all possible splits is NP-hard (practically impossible for big data), so we use a clever heuristic.

Idea 2: Lloyd's algorithm

The idea

Lloyd's algorithm (what people usually mean by "k-means")

  1. Initialise kkk centres (random points, or smarter: see k-means++).
  2. Assignment step. Send every point to its nearest centre: zi=arg⁡min⁡k∥xi−μk∥2z_i=\arg\min_k\|\mathbf x_i-\boldsymbol\mu_k\|^2zi​=argmink​∥xi​−μk​∥2.
  3. Update step. Move every centre to the mean of the points assigned to it: μk=1∣Ck∣∑i∈Ckxi\boldsymbol\mu_k=\frac1{|C_k|}\sum_{i\in C_k}\mathbf x_iμk​=∣Ck​∣1​∑i∈Ck​​xi​.
  4. Repeat 2 and 3 until the assignments stop changing.
Try it yourself

The three squares are the centres, placed badly on purpose. Press "Assign" then "Update" and watch them walk to the middle of their groups. The total squared distance can only go down.

Why it always stops (and where it stops)

Each step solves a small problem exactly:

  • Assignment step: if the centres are fixed, the best choice of ziz_izi​ for each point is clearly the nearest centre. So FFF cannot go up.
  • Update step: if the groups are fixed, the best centre for a group minimises ∑(xi−μ)2\sum(\mathbf x_i-\boldsymbol\mu)^2∑(xi​−μ)2. Setting the derivative to zero (MLF week 2) gives μ=\boldsymbol\mu=μ= the mean. So FFF cannot go up again.

So FFF never increases. And there are only finitely many ways to split nnn points into kkk groups, so the algorithm cannot keep improving forever: it must stop after a finite number of steps.

Watch out - real exam trap

Be precise about what is guaranteed. Lloyd's algorithm always converges, in finitely many steps, to a local minimum of FFF. It is not guaranteed to reach the global minimum, and different starting centres can give different final answers.

Idea 3: Starting well, k-means++

A bad start can trap Lloyd's algorithm (two centres inside the same blob, an empty blob left). k-means++ chooses starting centres that are spread out:

The idea
  1. Pick the first centre uniformly at random from the data.
  2. For every point x\mathbf xx, compute D(x)D(\mathbf x)D(x), its distance to the nearest centre already chosen.
  3. Pick the next centre from the data with probability proportional to D(x)2D(\mathbf x)^2D(x)2.
  4. Repeat until you have kkk centres. Then run Lloyd's.

Far-away points get a much bigger chance (squared distance), so new centres tend to land in uncovered regions. Guarantee: the expected objective is at most O(log⁡k)O(\log k)O(logk) times the best possible, specifically E[F]≤8(ln⁡k+2) FoptE[F]\le8(\ln k+2)\,F_{\text{opt}}E[F]≤8(lnk+2)Fopt​.

Idea 4: Choosing kkk, and what k-means assumes

  • FFF always decreases when you add clusters, reaching 000 at k=nk=nk=n (every point its own centre). So you cannot just pick the smallest FFF.
  • The elbow method: plot FFF against kkk and choose the "bend" where extra clusters stop helping much.
  • k-means works best when clusters are round, similar in size and well separated. Long thin or nested shapes (like rings) break it.
  • It is sensitive to scale (standardise features) and to outliers (a mean is dragged by extremes).
  • The centre is the mean because of the squared distance. Using absolute distance would give the median (k-medians).

Link to the next week: k-means is the hard-assignment limit of a Gaussian mixture model fitted with EM. Every point belongs to one cluster, instead of having fractional memberships.

Idea 5: Kernel k-means

For ring-shaped clusters, run k-means in the lifted space of MLT week 2. We cannot compute the centre μC=1∣C∣∑j∈Cϕ(xj)\boldsymbol\mu_C=\frac1{|C|}\sum_{j\in C}\phi(\mathbf x_j)μC​=∣C∣1​∑j∈C​ϕ(xj​) explicitly, but we do not need to: the squared distance from ϕ(x)\phi(\mathbf x)ϕ(x) to it only needs kernel values:

∥ϕ(x)−μC∥2=k(x,x)−2∣C∣∑j∈Ck(x,xj)+1∣C∣2∑j∈C∑l∈Ck(xj,xl)\|\phi(\mathbf x)-\boldsymbol\mu_C\|^2=k(\mathbf x,\mathbf x)-\frac2{|C|}\sum_{j\in C}k(\mathbf x,\mathbf x_j)+\frac1{|C|^2}\sum_{j\in C}\sum_{l\in C}k(\mathbf x_j,\mathbf x_l)∥ϕ(x)−μC​∥2=k(x,x)−∣C∣2​j∈C∑​k(x,xj​)+∣C∣21​j∈C∑​l∈C∑​k(xj​,xl​)

Then Lloyd's algorithm proceeds as before with this distance: assign each point to the cluster giving the smallest value; there is no explicit centre to update.

Watch out - real exam trap
  • Converges ≠\ne= globally optimal.
  • k-means++ probability is proportional to D(x)2D(\mathbf x)^2D(x)2 (squared), not D(x)D(\mathbf x)D(x).
  • The update uses the mean (squared distance). With absolute distance it would be the median.
  • FFF computed after assignment and after update are different numbers; read which one is asked.
  • More clusters always lowers FFF; don't choose kkk by minimising FFF.
