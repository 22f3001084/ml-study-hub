Week 6 · Ridge and LASSO: taming overfitting

Read the words of the video
  1. Imagine a very eager student changing their answers to fit every little detail of practice questions, even noise. They fit practice data beautifully but fail on new questions. Our computer models can do this too, learning too much noise. We give the model a 'leash' to keep its answers sensible.
  2. We add a penalty for large 'weights' – the numbers the model learns. Ridge regression uses a soft leash: it gently pulls all weights smaller, but never to zero. LASSO regression uses a sharp leash: it can pull some weights exactly to zero, turning off unhelpful features.
  3. Let's see an example. If a model's best 'weight' for a feature is 3 without a penalty. Ridge, with a penalty, will shrink it, maybe to 1 or 0.3, but always keeps it positive. LASSO, however, with a strong enough penalty, can make that weight exactly zero, completely removing that feature.
  4. For your exam, remember that Ridge never makes weights exactly zero; LASSO can. Both add penalties to stop models from learning noise and overfitting. We choose the right 'leash strength', called lambda, by finding what works best on new data. Great job learning this complex idea!

The one-minute story

Think of it like this

A model with too much freedom is like a very eager student who changes their answer to fit every little detail of the practice questions, including noise. Their weights (the numbers the model learns) swing wildly: +900 here, −870 there, cancelling each other out. It fits the practice data beautifully and falls apart on a new question.

The cure is a leash on the weights. We add a penalty for large weights to the thing we are minimising. Now the model must be useful and modest.

  • Ridge puts a soft leash: every weight is pulled gently towards zero, but none is ever cut loose entirely.
  • LASSO puts a sharp leash: it can pull some weights exactly to zero, which switches those features off. It chooses which features matter.

Same idea, two flavours, two different personalities.

By the end of this week you can:

  • explain overfitting and why large weights are a warning sign;
  • derive and use the ridge solution (XTX+λI)−1XTy(X^TX+\lambda I)^{-1}X^T\mathbf y(XTX+λI)−1XTy;
  • explain LASSO and why it gives sparse weights;
  • interpret both as MAP estimation (Gaussian prior vs Laplace prior);
  • pick λ\lambdaλ by cross-validation and reason about bias and variance.

Words you need

Words you need
  • Regularisation - adding a penalty to discourage complicated models.
  • λ\lambdaλ - the penalty strength. λ=0\lambda=0λ=0 means no penalty.
  • ℓ2\ell_2ℓ2​ penalty ∥w∥2=∑wj2\|\mathbf w\|^2=\sum w_j^2∥w∥2=∑wj2​ (ridge). ℓ1\ell_1ℓ1​ penalty ∥w∥1=∑∣wj∣\|\mathbf w\|_1=\sum|w_j|∥w∥1​=∑∣wj​∣ (LASSO).
  • Shrinkage - making the weights smaller than the least-squares ones.
  • Sparse - mostly zeros.
  • Bias - systematic error from a model being too simple. Variance - sensitivity of the model to the particular training sample.

Idea 1: Why penalise?

If two features are almost copies of each other, least squares can give them huge opposite weights that nearly cancel. If XTXX^TXXTX is singular (columns dependent) it is even worse: there are infinitely many equally good solutions. Penalising the size of the weights fixes both problems: it prefers small, stable weights.

Idea 2: Ridge regression (ℓ2\ell_2ℓ2​ penalty)

Minimise fit + penalty:

Lridge(w)=∥y−Xw∥2+λ∥w∥2\mathcal L_{\text{ridge}}(\mathbf w)=\|\mathbf y-X\mathbf w\|^2+\lambda\|\mathbf w\|^2Lridge​(w)=∥y−Xw∥2+λ∥w∥2

Take the gradient and set it to zero: −2XT(y−Xw)+2λw=0-2X^T(\mathbf y-X\mathbf w)+2\lambda\mathbf w=0−2XT(y−Xw)+2λw=0, giving

wridge=(XTX+λI)−1XTy\boxed{\mathbf w_{\text{ridge}}=(X^TX+\lambda I)^{-1}X^T\mathbf y}wridge​=(XTX+λI)−1XTy​

Things to notice:

  • Adding λI\lambda IλI adds λ\lambdaλ to every eigenvalue of XTXX^TXXTX, so for any λ>0\lambda>0λ>0 the matrix is invertible, even if XTXX^TXXTX was singular. Ridge always has a unique answer.
  • λ→0\lambda\to0λ→0: back to least squares. λ→∞\lambda\to\inftyλ→∞: w→0\mathbf w\to\mathbf 0w→0.
  • The objective is strictly convex (MLF week 8) so there is exactly one minimum.
  • In the simplest case of one feature, wridge=∑xiyi∑xi2+λ\mathbf w_{\text{ridge}}=\dfrac{\sum x_iy_i}{\sum x_i^2+\lambda}wridge​=∑xi2​+λ∑xi​yi​​: the penalty just inflates the denominator, shrinking www.

Idea 3: LASSO (ℓ1\ell_1ℓ1​ penalty)

Llasso(w)=∥y−Xw∥2+λ∥w∥1\mathcal L_{\text{lasso}}(\mathbf w)=\|\mathbf y-X\mathbf w\|^2+\lambda\|\mathbf w\|_1Llasso​(w)=∥y−Xw∥2+λ∥w∥1​

The absolute value has a sharp corner at 0 so the objective is not differentiable there, and there is no closed form in general (we use iterative methods). But it has a famous property: it sets some weights exactly to 0, a built-in feature selector.

Try it yourself

Both curves are (w−3)2(w-3)^2(w−3)2 plus a penalty. Drag lambda. Ridge's minimum slides smoothly towards 0 but never touches it; LASSO's minimum hits 0 and stays there once the penalty is big enough.

wlasso=sign⁡(z) max⁡ ⁣(∣z∣−λ2,  0)w_{\text{lasso}}=\operatorname{sign}(z)\,\max\!\Big(|z|-\frac\lambda2,\;0\Big)wlasso​=sign(z)max(∣z∣−2λ​,0)

and the ridge version of the same problem, (w−z)2+λw2(w-z)^2+\lambda w^2(w−z)2+λw2, gives wridge=z1+λw_{\text{ridge}}=\dfrac z{1+\lambda}wridge​=1+λz​.

The geometry (why LASSO makes zeros)

Equivalent view: minimise the squared error subject to a budget on the weights: ∥w∥22≤c\|\mathbf w\|_2^2\le c∥w∥22​≤c (ridge) or ∥w∥1≤c\|\mathbf w\|_1\le c∥w∥1​≤c (LASSO). The error has elliptical contour lines. The ridge budget region is a round disc; the LASSO region is a diamond with corners on the axes. The first contour to touch a smooth disc touches it anywhere; but it very often first touches the diamond at a corner, and a corner sits on an axis, where one weight is exactly 0.

Idea 4: The Bayesian meaning

From MLT week 5: Gaussian noise gives the squared-error fit term. Add a prior on the weights:

Gaussian N(0,τ2)\mathcal N(0,\tau^2)N(0,τ2)squared error +λ∥w∥22+\lambda\|\mathbf w\|_2^2+λ∥w∥22​, λ=σ2/τ2\lambda=\sigma^2/\tau^2λ=σ2/τ2Ridge
Laplace ∝e−∣w∣/b\propto e^{-|w|/b}∝e−∣w∣/bsquared error +λ∥w∥1+\lambda\|\mathbf w\|_1+λ∥w∥1​LASSO

A Laplace density has a sharp spike at 0, so it "believes in zeros", which explains the sparsity. A tighter prior (small τ\tauτ) means a bigger λ\lambdaλ.

Idea 5: Choosing λ\lambdaλ and the bias-variance trade-off

  • Small λ\lambdaλ: flexible, low bias, high variance (overfits).
  • Large λ\lambdaλ: stiff, high bias, low variance (underfits).
  • Training error rises with λ\lambdaλ; validation error is U-shaped: best somewhere in the middle. Pick λ\lambdaλ by cross-validation.
  • Standardise the features first (the penalty treats all weights equally, so features on different scales would be punished unfairly), and normally do not penalise the intercept.
  • Elastic net mixes both penalties.

Exam radar

Exam radar

MLT Quiz 2 summaries mention ridge in 7 papers. In the End Term: ridge regression in 20 papers (and "Ridge Regression (L2 Regularization)" in 13), overfitting in 19, underfitting in 15. LASSO is named in the course syllabus, so expect it too.

Typical: "which of the following is the ridge solution?", "what happens to the weights as λ→∞\lambda\to\inftyλ→∞?", "which regulariser produces sparse weights?", "ridge as MAP with which prior?", and a one-dimensional ridge calculation.

Watch out - real exam trap
  • Ridge never gives exact zeros; LASSO can.
  • Ridge penalty is on squared weights; the intercept is usually not penalised.
  • Bigger λ\lambdaλ means more bias, less variance.
  • Gaussian prior ↔\leftrightarrow↔ ridge; Laplace prior ↔\leftrightarrow↔ LASSO (easy to swap by accident).
  • Features must be on comparable scales before regularising.

Recap

Remember this
  • Ridge: ∥y−Xw∥2+λ∥w∥2⇒w=(XTX+λI)−1XTy\|\mathbf y-X\mathbf w\|^2+\lambda\|\mathbf w\|^2\Rightarrow\mathbf w=(X^TX+\lambda I)^{-1}X^T\mathbf y∥y−Xw∥2+λ∥w∥2⇒w=(XTX+λI)−1XTy. Always invertible; shrinks smoothly; Gaussian prior.
  • LASSO: ∥y−Xw∥2+λ∥w∥1\|\mathbf y-X\mathbf w\|^2+\lambda\|\mathbf w\|_1∥y−Xw∥2+λ∥w∥1​. Sparse weights; no closed form; Laplace prior; soft-threshold in 1-D.
  • λ↑\lambda\uparrowλ↑: bias ↑\uparrow↑, variance ↓\downarrow↓, training error ↑\uparrow↑. Choose λ\lambdaλ by cross-validation; standardise features.
