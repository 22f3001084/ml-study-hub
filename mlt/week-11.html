Read the words of the video
  1. Imagine asking one friend for advice; they might be wrong. But if you ask many friends, and they all give slightly different opinions, you often get a better answer. This is like combining many simple ideas to make one really strong one. We call this an ensemble.
  2. There are two main ways to build this committee. Imagine many students studying different notes, then voting. This is called Bagging, and it helps fix 'jumpy' mistakes. Or, imagine tutors teaching one after another, each focusing on what the last one got wrong. This is Boosting, which makes 'weak' lessons strong.
  3. With Boosting, we focus on tough problems. Imagine five balloons, all equally weighted. If our first attempt pops just one balloon, that tricky balloon instantly becomes much heavier. So, the next attempt will try extra hard to avoid that specific balloon, learning from the mistake.
  4. Remember, for exams, know how Bagging fixes 'jumpy' problems by reducing variance, and how Boosting strengthens 'weak' lessons by reducing bias. Also, roughly 37 percent of data is 'out-of-bag' in Bagging. Keep practicing these ideas, and you'll do great!

The one-minute story

Think of it like this

One expert may be wrong. A committee is harder to fool. An ensemble combines many simple models into one strong one. There are two classic ways to build the committee:

Bagging: many students, each studying a different random pile of the notes. Each student gives a vote, and we go with the majority. Because each saw slightly different notes, their mistakes point in different directions and cancel out. This calms jumpy models (variance), like deep decision trees.

Boosting: a relay of tutors. Tutor 1 teaches a quick lesson and finds which questions the class got wrong. Tutor 2 concentrates specifically on those hard questions. Tutor 3 does the same for what is still wrong, and so on. The final answer is a weighted vote where better tutors speak louder. This lifts weak models (bias) into a strong one. AdaBoost is the famous example.

By the end of this week you can:

  • explain why averaging reduces variance;
  • describe bootstrap sampling, bagging, out-of-bag error and random forests;
  • run AdaBoost by hand: weights, error ε\varepsilonε, α\alphaα, reweighting;
  • compare bagging and boosting.

Words you need

Words you need
  • Ensemble - a group of models whose predictions are combined.
  • Weak learner - a model only slightly better than guessing (a one-question decision tree, a "stump").
  • Bootstrap sample - a sample of size nnn drawn with replacement from nnn examples.
  • Out-of-bag (OOB) - the examples a given bootstrap sample missed.
  • Random forest - bagged decision trees with extra randomness in the features each split may use.
  • Boosting - training models in sequence, each focusing on the previous ones' mistakes.

Idea 1: Why averaging helps

Suppose nnn models each make a prediction with variance σ2\sigma^2σ2, and their errors are independent. The average has variance

Var⁡(1n∑f^i)=σ2n\operatorname{Var}\Big(\frac1n\sum\hat f_i\Big)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}Var(n1​∑f^​i​)=nσ2​

Ten independent models with variance 444 give an average with variance 0.40.40.4. If the errors are correlated with correlation ρ\rhoρ, the variance is ρσ2+1−ρnσ2\rho\sigma^2+\frac{1-\rho}n\sigma^2ρσ2+n1−ρ​σ2: averaging helps less when the models are alike. So the art is to make models that are good but different.

Idea 2: Bagging and random forests

The idea

Bagging (Bootstrap aggregating):

  1. Build BBB bootstrap samples (each: nnn draws with replacement from the training set).
  2. Train one model (typically a deep, unpruned decision tree) on each.
  3. Predict by majority vote (classification) or average (regression).

It reduces variance and leaves the bias about the same. It works best with high-variance, low-bias models (deep trees) and does little for stable ones.

Out-of-bag error. The chance a particular example is not picked in a bootstrap sample is (1−1n)n→e−1≈0.368\big(1-\frac1n\big)^n\to e^{-1}\approx0.368(1−n1​)n→e−1≈0.368. So each model never sees about 36.8% of the data. Test each model on the examples it missed and average: a free, honest validation estimate with no separate hold-out set.

Random forest = bagged trees plus one trick: at every split, the tree may only consider a random subset of the features (about d\sqrt dd​ for classification). That forces the trees to differ (ρ\rhoρ smaller), so the average is better. Forests are strong, need little tuning and give a feature importance measure.

Solved example: 1. Out-of-bag fraction

Question. A training set has n=1000n=1000n=1000 examples. About how many are out-of-bag for one bootstrap sample?

Solution. (1−11000)1000≈e−1=0.368\big(1-\frac1{1000}\big)^{1000}\approx e^{-1}=0.368(1−10001​)1000≈e−1=0.368, so about 368 examples are left out (and about 632 distinct ones are used).

Idea 3: Boosting and AdaBoost

AdaBoost (Adaptive Boosting) keeps a weight on every training example, and re-weights after every round so that the next learner concentrates on the examples the ensemble currently gets wrong. Labels are ±1\pm1±1 and each weak learner hth_tht​ outputs ±1\pm1±1.

The idea

AdaBoost

  1. Start with equal weights wi=1nw_i=\frac1nwi​=n1​.

  2. For rounds t=1,…,Tt=1,\dots,Tt=1,…,T:

    • train a weak learner hth_tht​ using the weights wiw_iwi​;
    • compute its weighted error εt=∑i: ht(xi)≠yiwi\varepsilon_t=\sum_{i:\,h_t(\mathbf x_i)\ne y_i}w_iεt​=∑i:ht​(xi​)=yi​​wi​ (must be <0.5<0.5<0.5);
    • its say in the final vote: αt=12ln⁡1−εtεt\alpha_t=\dfrac12\ln\dfrac{1-\varepsilon_t}{\varepsilon_t}αt​=21​lnεt​1−εt​​;
    • update: wi←wi e−αtyiht(xi)w_i\leftarrow w_i\,e^{-\alpha_ty_ih_t(\mathbf x_i)}wi​←wi​e−αt​yi​ht​(xi​) (wrong examples get heavier, right ones lighter), then normalise so the weights sum to 1.
  3. Final classifier: H(x)=sign⁡ ⁣(∑t=1Tαtht(x))H(\mathbf x)=\operatorname{sign}\!\Big(\sum_{t=1}^T\alpha_th_t(\mathbf x)\Big)H(x)=sign(∑t=1T​αt​ht​(x)).

Intuition for αt\alpha_tαt​: εt\varepsilon_tεt​ close to 0 means a great learner, αt\alpha_tαt​ is large; εt=0.5\varepsilon_t=0.5εt​=0.5 means a useless coin flip, αt=0\alpha_t=0αt​=0; εt>0.5\varepsilon_t>0.5εt​>0.5 would give a negative weight (flip its vote).

Solved example: 2. One AdaBoost round

Question. Five examples start with weights 0.20.20.2 each. The first stump gets exactly one wrong. Compute ε1\varepsilon_1ε1​, α1\alpha_1α1​ and the new weights.

Solution.

  1. ε1=0.2\varepsilon_1=0.2ε1​=0.2 (the weight of the one mistake).
  2. α1=12ln⁡0.80.2=12ln⁡4=0.693\alpha_1=\frac12\ln\frac{0.8}{0.2}=\frac12\ln4=\mathbf{0.693}α1​=21​ln0.20.8​=21​ln4=0.693.
  3. Wrong example: 0.2⋅e0.693=0.2⋅2=0.40.2\cdot e^{0.693}=0.2\cdot2=0.40.2⋅e0.693=0.2⋅2=0.4. Each of the four right ones: 0.2⋅e−0.693=0.2⋅0.5=0.10.2\cdot e^{-0.693}=0.2\cdot0.5=0.10.2⋅e−0.693=0.2⋅0.5=0.1. Sum =0.4+4(0.1)=0.8=0.4+4(0.1)=0.8=0.4+4(0.1)=0.8.
  4. Normalise (divide by 0.80.80.8): wrong example =0.5=\mathbf{0.5}=0.5; each right one =0.125=\mathbf{0.125}=0.125.

The single mistake now holds half of all the weight. The next stump will be heavily penalised unless it gets that example right, which is exactly the "focus on the hard question" idea.

Solved example: 3. Combining weak learners

Question. Three stumps with α=(0.9, 0.4, 0.3)\alpha=(0.9,\,0.4,\,0.3)α=(0.9,0.4,0.3) vote +1, −1, −1+1,\,-1,\,-1+1,−1,−1 on a new point. What does the final classifier say?

Solution. Score =0.9(+1)+0.4(−1)+0.3(−1)=0.2>0=0.9(+1)+0.4(-1)+0.3(-1)=0.2>0=0.9(+1)+0.4(−1)+0.3(−1)=0.2>0, so the prediction is +1+1+1. Two stumps said −1-1−1, but the first is trusted more than the other two together.

Facts about AdaBoost:

  • Only needs weak learners with εt<0.5\varepsilon_t<0.5εt​<0.5; the training error drops exponentially with the number of rounds (bounded by ∏t2εt(1−εt)\prod_t2\sqrt{\varepsilon_t(1-\varepsilon_t)}∏t​2εt​(1−εt​)​).
  • It reduces bias: many simple stumps combine into a complex boundary.
  • It is sensitive to noise and outliers: mislabelled points keep getting heavier and can drag the ensemble along, so it can overfit noisy data.
  • It minimises an exponential loss; gradient boosting generalises the idea to any differentiable loss.

Idea 4: Bagging versus boosting

Recap
