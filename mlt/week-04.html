The one-minute story

Think of it like this

Part 1, one coin. You find a coin and flip it 10 times: 7 heads. What is the chance of heads? A quick answer is "70%". But what if it looked like a perfectly ordinary coin? You would probably say "it is surely close to 50%, 7 out of 10 is just luck". The data and your prior feeling should both count. Bayesian estimation is the maths of mixing the two: start with a belief, update it with data, and end with a better belief. With lots of data the evidence wins; with little data the prior still has a voice.

Part 2, mixed groups. Measure the heights of 1000 people at a school gathering, but forget to write down who is a child and who is an adult. The heights form two overlapping bumps. A Gaussian mixture model (GMM) says: "each person comes from one of two hidden groups, and each group has its own bell curve". EM (expectation-maximisation) learns the two bell curves without ever being told the groups, by alternately guessing the groups and refitting the curves.

By the end of this week you can:

  • compute MLE for common distributions and explain its bias;
  • do Bayesian estimation with a Beta prior and a Bernoulli likelihood, and tell MLE, MAP and posterior mean apart;
  • write the GMM model and compute its log-likelihood;
  • carry out the E-step and M-step by hand and count GMM parameters.

MLE is the pure "let the data speak" answer. With 3 tosses and 3 heads it says p^=1\hat p=1p^​=1: "this coin never shows tails", which is silly. That is where a prior helps.

Idea 2: Bayesian estimation

Treat the unknown parameter as a random variable with a distribution. Bayes' rule (MLF week 10) says

p(θ∣data)⏟posterior  ∝  p(data∣θ)⏟likelihood  ×  p(θ)⏟prior\underbrace{p(\theta\mid\text{data})}_{\text{posterior}}\;\propto\;\underbrace{p(\text{data}\mid\theta)}_{\text{likelihood}}\;\times\;\underbrace{p(\theta)}_{\text{prior}}posteriorp(θ∣data)​​∝likelihoodp(data∣θ)​​×priorp(θ)​​

The Beta-Bernoulli model: the most-asked calculation of this quiz

The unknown is a coin's heads-probability θ∈[0,1]\theta\in[0,1]θ∈[0,1]. A natural prior is the Beta distribution:

θ∼Beta(α,β),p(θ)∝θα−1(1−θ)β−1\theta\sim\text{Beta}(\alpha,\beta),\qquad p(\theta)\propto\theta^{\alpha-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta-1}θ∼Beta(α,β),p(θ)∝θα−1(1−θ)β−1

with mean αα+β\dfrac\alpha{\alpha+\beta}α+βα​. You see hhh heads and ttt tails (n=h+tn=h+tn=h+t). The likelihood is θh(1−θ)t\theta^h(1-\theta)^tθh(1−θ)t, so

p(θ∣data)∝θα−1+h(1−θ)β−1+t⟹θ∣data∼Beta(α+h,  β+t)p(\theta\mid\text{data})\propto\theta^{\alpha-1+h}(1-\theta)^{\beta-1+t}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\boxed{\theta\mid\text{data}\sim\text{Beta}(\alpha+h,\;\beta+t)}p(θ∣data)∝θα−1+h(1−θ)β−1+t⟹θ∣data∼Beta(α+h,β+t)​

That is conjugacy: the posterior is the same kind of curve as the prior, with the counts simply added.

Think of it like this

Read α\alphaα and β\betaβ as pretend flips you already saw: α\alphaα pretend heads and β\betaβ pretend tails. Beta(2,2) means "I have a hunch from 2 heads and 2 tails". Then real data just adds real flips to the pile. After the update you have a pile of α+h\alpha+hα+h heads and β+t\beta+tβ+t tails, and the Beta curve for that pile is your new belief.

Try it yourself

Set a prior, add data, and see the posterior (orange). With more data, the curve gets narrower and moves from the prior towards the MLE.

Observations:

  • The posterior mean is a blend: α+hα+β+n\frac{\alpha+h}{\alpha+\beta+n}α+β+nα+h​ lies between the prior mean and the MLE, and moves towards the MLE as nnn grows.

  • As n→∞n\to\inftyn→∞ the prior washes out: MAP, posterior mean and MLE all converge.
Solved example: 1. Beta posterior

Question. Prior Beta(2,2). You see 7 heads and 3 tails. Give the posterior, its mean, the MAP estimate and the MLE. What is the probability the next toss is heads?

Idea 3: Gaussian mixture models

A GMM says each data point is generated in two stages:

  1. Pick a component k∈{1,…,K}k\in\{1,\dots,K\}k∈{1,…,K} with probability πk\pi_kπk​ (the mixing weights, ∑πk=1\sum\pi_k=1∑πk​=1).
  2. Draw x\mathbf xx from N(μk,Σk)\mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu_k,\Sigma_k)N(μk​,Σk​).

The density of a point is the weighted sum

p(x)=∑k=1Kπk N(x∣μk,Σk)p(\mathbf x)=\sum_{k=1}^K\pi_k\,\mathcal N(\mathbf x\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\Sigma_k)p(x)=k=1∑K​πk​N(x∣μk​,Σk​)

and for nnn independent points the log-likelihood is

ℓ=∑i=1nln⁡(∑k=1KπkN(xi∣μk,Σk))\ell=\sum_{i=1}^n\ln\Big(\sum_{k=1}^K\pi_k\mathcal N(\mathbf x_i\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\Sigma_k)\Big)ℓ=i=1∑n​ln(k=1∑K​πk​N(xi​∣μk​,Σk​))

The log of a sum blocks a neat formula for the best parameters, so we use EM.

The EM algorithm for a GMM

The idea

E-step. For every point and every component, the responsibility

rik=πk N(xi∣μk,Σk)∑j=1Kπj N(xi∣μj,Σj)r_{ik}=\frac{\pi_k\,\mathcal N(\mathbf x_i\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\Sigma_k)}{\sum_{j=1}^K\pi_j\,\mathcal N(\mathbf x_i\mid\boldsymbol\mu_j,\Sigma_j)}rik​=∑j=1K​πj​N(xi​∣μj​,Σj​)πk​N(xi​∣μk​,Σk​)​

(Each point's responsibilities add to 1 across components.)

M-step. With nk=∑irikn_k=\sum_ir_{ik}nk​=∑i​rik​ (the "effective number of points" in component kkk):

πk=nkn,μk=1nk∑irikxi,Σk=1nk∑irik(xi−μk)(xi−μk)T\pi_k=\frac{n_k}n,\qquad\boldsymbol\mu_k=\frac1{n_k}\sum_ir_{ik}\mathbf x_i,\qquad\Sigma_k=\frac1{n_k}\sum_ir_{ik}(\mathbf x_i-\boldsymbol\mu_k)(\mathbf x_i-\boldsymbol\mu_k)^Tπk​=nnk​​,μk​=nk​1​i∑​rik​xi​,Σk​=nk​1​i∑​rik​(xi​−μk​)(xi​−μk​)T

These are exactly the ordinary MLE formulas for a Gaussian, but every point is weighted by its responsibility.

Properties worth knowing:

  • Each EM iteration never decreases the log-likelihood; the algorithm climbs to a local maximum (it can depend on the start, which is often set by k-means).
  • If one component collapses onto a single point, its variance →0\to0→0 and the likelihood →∞\to\infty→∞: a singularity that must be avoided (add a small floor to the variance).
  • k-means is the hard version: shrink every Σk\Sigma_kΣk​ to σ2I\sigma^2Iσ2I and let σ2→0\sigma^2\to0σ2→0; the responsibilities become 0 or 1 and EM becomes Lloyd's algorithm.

Watch out - real exam trap
  • Mean vs mode of the posterior; the mode has the −1-1−1 and −2-2−2.
  • Prior pseudo-counts are α\alphaα and β\betaβ (not α−1\alpha-1α−1) when you compute the posterior mean.
  • The Beta prior is for the heads-probability, so adding data adds heads to α\alphaα and tails to β\betaβ.
  • Responsibilities are per point, summing over components, not over points.
  • EM gives a local maximum. It does not guarantee the best fit.
  • The Gaussian-variance MLE divides by nnn.
