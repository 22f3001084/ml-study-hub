Read the words of the video
  1. Imagine you're trying to guess the price of a house. You look at its size, how many rooms it has, and compare it to other houses you know. This is like 'supervised learning' where you learn from examples with correct answers. We want to find a simple rule, like a line, to make good guesses.
  2. We find this 'best line' by making the total 'mistake' as small as possible. Imagine the line is like a target, and each data point is an arrow. We measure how far each arrow misses the target. We then square these misses and add them up. The line that gives the smallest total squared miss is our best guess!
  3. Let's try! If we have points like (zero, one), (one, two), and (two, two), our best line might be 'y equals seven-sixths plus one-half x'. To predict for a new input, say 'x equals three', we just plug it into our line's rule. The line helps us make educated guesses for new data!
  4. Remember, we use a 'line' to predict numbers, finding it by making total squared errors smallest. This is linked to 'Gaussian noise' for exams! Always test your line on new, unseen data to see how well it truly performs, not just on the data it learned from. Keep practicing, you've got this!

The one-minute story

Think of it like this

Supervised learning means learning with a teacher: every example comes with the right answer. This week the answer is a number (a house price, a temperature, tomorrow's sales). That is regression.

You have met the idea before: draw the best straight line through a cloud of dots. We now look at it three ways:

  1. as geometry (a shadow, from MLF week 3),
  2. as calculus (make the total squared mistake as small as possible),
  3. as probability (assume the data is the line plus random noise, and find the most likely line).

All three give the same line. The probability view is new, and it opens a door: once you can say what kind of noise you expect, you can also say what kind of line you believe in beforehand, which is the Bayesian view and leads straight to next week's ridge regression.

By the end of this week you can:

  • write linear regression with a design matrix and solve the normal equations;
  • explain least squares as projection, as gradient descent, and as MLE under Gaussian noise;
  • use basis functions (polynomial regression) to fit curves with a linear model;
  • measure quality with MSE and validate with a held-out set or leave-one-out cross-validation.

Idea 1: Linear regression

Model: y^=wTx\hat y=\mathbf w^T\mathbf xy^​=wTx (add a constant 1 to x\mathbf xx to get an intercept). For all nnn examples at once, with the design matrix XXX whose rows are the xiT\mathbf x_i^TxiT​:

y^=Xw\hat{\mathbf y}=X\mathbf wy^​=Xw

Loss. The sum of squared errors:

L(w)=∑i=1n(yi−wTxi)2=∥y−Xw∥2\mathcal L(\mathbf w)=\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_i)^2=\|\mathbf y-X\mathbf w\|^2L(w)=i=1∑n​(yi​−wTxi​)2=∥y−Xw∥2

Solving it (calculus). The gradient is −2XT(y−Xw)-2X^T(\mathbf y-X\mathbf w)−2XT(y−Xw). Setting it to zero:

XTX w=XTy⟹w∗=(XTX)−1XTy\boxed{X^TX\,\mathbf w=X^T\mathbf y}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\mathbf w^*=(X^TX)^{-1}X^T\mathbf yXTXw=XTy​⟹w∗=(XTX)−1XTy

These are the normal equations. Geometrically: Xw∗X\mathbf w^*Xw∗ is the projection of y\mathbf yy onto the column space of XXX, and the residual y−Xw∗\mathbf y-X\mathbf w^*y−Xw∗ is perpendicular to every column of XXX (MLF week 3). By iteration: you can also reach w∗\mathbf w^*w∗ by gradient descent, w←w+2ηXT(y−Xw)\mathbf w\leftarrow\mathbf w+2\eta X^T(\mathbf y-X\mathbf w)w←w+2ηXT(y−Xw) (MLF week 7). With millions of examples that is how it is done.

If the columns of XXX are linearly dependent, XTXX^TXXTX is not invertible and the best w\mathbf ww is not unique. That is exactly the situation ridge regression fixes next week.

Idea 2: The probabilistic view

Suppose the world works like this:

yi=wTxi+εi,εi∼N(0,σ2) independentlyy_i=\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_i+\varepsilon_i,\qquad\varepsilon_i\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)\ \text{independently}yi​=wTxi​+εi​,εi​∼N(0,σ2) independently

i.e. a true line plus bell-shaped noise. Then yi∣xi∼N(wTxi,σ2)y_i\mid\mathbf x_i\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_i,\sigma^2)yi​∣xi​∼N(wTxi​,σ2), and the log-likelihood of the data is

ℓ(w)=−12σ2∑i(yi−wTxi)2+constant\ell(\mathbf w)=-\frac1{2\sigma^2}\sum_i(y_i-\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_i)^2+\text{constant}ℓ(w)=−2σ21​i∑​(yi​−wTxi​)2+constant

Maximising ℓ\ellℓ means minimising the sum of squared errors. So:

The idea

Least squares === maximum likelihood estimation under Gaussian noise.

This is why squared error is the "natural" loss for regression: it is what you get when the noise is bell-shaped.

The Bayesian view (a preview of ridge)

Now put a prior on the weights too: w∼N(0,τ2I)\mathbf w\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\tau^2I)w∼N(0,τ2I), "I expect the weights to be small". Then (week 4's recipe: posterior ∝\propto∝ likelihood ×\times× prior) the MAP estimate minimises

∑i(yi−wTxi)2+λ∥w∥2,λ=σ2τ2\sum_i(y_i-\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_i)^2+\lambda\|\mathbf w\|^2,\qquad\lambda=\frac{\sigma^2}{\tau^2}i∑​(yi​−wTxi​)2+λ∥w∥2,λ=τ2σ2​

That is ridge regression, which we study next week. Least squares is the special case with an infinitely wide (uninformative) prior.

Idea 3: Curves from lines, using basis functions

"Linear" means linear in the weights, not in xxx. To fit a curve, replace xxx with features such as ϕ(x)=(1, x, x2, …, xp)\phi(x)=(1,\,x,\,x^2,\,\dots,\,x^p)ϕ(x)=(1,x,x2,…,xp). The model y^=w0+w1x+⋯+wpxp\hat y=w_0+w_1x+\dots+w_px^py^​=w0​+w1​x+⋯+wp​xp is still linear in w\mathbf ww, so all the machinery above applies with the new design matrix.

  • Underfitting: ppp too small, the curve is too stiff to follow the pattern (high bias).
  • Overfitting: ppp too large, the curve wiggles through every noisy dot and fails on new data (high variance). With p=n−1p=n-1p=n−1 the polynomial passes through all nnn points exactly and training error is 000, yet predictions can be wild.
Think of it like this

Fitting a curve through every dot is like a student who memorises the answers to last year's paper, including its printing mistakes. The score on that paper is perfect; on the new paper it is a disaster. We want the student who understood the pattern.

Idea 4: Checking quality honestly

MSE =1n∑(yi−y^i)2=\frac1n\sum(y_i-\hat y_i)^2=n1​∑(yi​−y^​i​)2. R2R^2R2 =1−∑(yi−y^i)2∑(yi−yˉ)2=1-\frac{\sum(y_i-\hat y_i)^2}{\sum(y_i-\bar y)^2}=1−∑(yi​−yˉ​)2∑(yi​−y^​i​)2​: the fraction of the variation explained (1 is perfect, 0 is as good as predicting the mean).

Training error always shrinks as the model gets more flexible. So judge on data the model has not seen:

  • Hold-out / validation set: keep a slice aside.
  • Leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV): for each example iii, train on the other n−1n-1n−1, predict example iii, record the squared error; average over all nnn. Accurate but costly (it needs nnn fits).
  • kkk-fold CV: split into kkk parts, each used once as validation.

Idea 5: Kernel regression, briefly

Just as in kernel PCA, a linear regression only needs dot products between inputs, so we can swap them for a kernel k(x,x′)k(\mathbf x,\mathbf x')k(x,x′) and fit curvy functions without ever building the features. The prediction takes the form y^(x)=∑iαik(xi,x)\hat y(\mathbf x)=\sum_i\alpha_ik(\mathbf x_i,\mathbf x)y^​(x)=∑i​αi​k(xi​,x), a weighted sum of "similarity to training point iii".

Watch out - real exam trap
  • Normal equations: XTXw=XTyX^TX\mathbf w=X^T\mathbf yXTXw=XTy (not XXTXX^TXXT). The shape of XXX matters (n×dn\times dn×d rows are examples).
  • Training error is not a good measure of quality; validation error is.
  • Squared-error corresponds to Gaussian noise.
  • A lower training MSE does not mean a better model if it overfits.
  • Don't forget the intercept column of ones.

Quick quiz

  • X transpose X w = X transpose y
  • w = y
  • X transpose w = X y
The normal equations come from setting the gradient of the squared error to zero.

Least squares is the maximum likelihood estimate when the noise is...

  • uniform
  • Gaussian
  • exponential
  • Poisson
Gaussian noise gives a log-likelihood proportional to the negative sum of squared errors.

A polynomial of degree n-1 fitted to n distinct points has training error...

  • always large
  • zero
  • equal to the variance
  • negative
It can pass exactly through all n points, so the training error is zero (and it usually overfits).
