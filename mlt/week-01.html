Week 1 · PCA: finding the best view of your data

Read the words of the video
  1. Imagine taking a photo of a cool 3D statue for a flat postcard. Most pictures make it look squashed or boring. But there's one special angle where the statue looks its widest and clearest, so you can still easily tell what it is. PCA is like finding that perfect angle for your data.
  2. Imagine describing each student with 100 quiz marks. That's too many numbers! Many quizzes might even measure similar things. PCA helps us find just a few new numbers, like two or three, that capture most of the important information. It finds directions where our data changes the most, keeping only what truly matters.
  3. Let's see how much information we keep! If our data has 'spread' values of five, three, and two, the total spread is ten. Keeping the first direction explains 50% of the spread. Keeping the first two directions explains 80%. So, to keep at least 75% of the information, we need two directions!
  4. For exams, remember to always 'centre' your data first! And, reconstruction error means the information you *discarded*, not what you kept. PCA is unsupervised, finding hidden patterns. It picks directions with the most 'spread,' making complex data simple and clear, like finding the best view of a statue!

The one-minute story

Think of it like this

A museum wants to put a 3D statue on a postcard, which is flat. Photograph it from the front, the top, the side... Most angles make the statue look squashed and boring. But one angle shows it spread out as widely as possible, so you can still tell what it is. PCA finds that best angle for any cloud of data.

Now imagine each student is described by 100 numbers (marks in 100 quizzes). You cannot picture a point in 100 dimensions, and many of the quizzes say nearly the same thing. PCA finds a handful of new numbers (maybe 2 or 3) that keep most of the information. Fewer numbers means faster models, cleaner pictures and less noise.

The idea in one line: look for the directions along which the data varies the most, and keep those.

The idea

Quick refresher from MLF (skip if fresh).

  • A projection onto a direction w\mathbf ww (unit length) gives the coordinate wTx\mathbf w^T\mathbf xwTx; the "shadow" is (wTx)w(\mathbf w^T\mathbf x)\mathbf w(wTx)w.
  • A symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues and perpendicular eigenvectors.
  • wTCw\mathbf w^TC\mathbf wwTCw is a quadratic form; for the top eigenvector it equals the largest eigenvalue.

By the end of this week you can:

  • explain what unsupervised learning and representation learning mean;
  • run PCA step by step: centre, covariance, eigen-analysis, project;
  • compute variance explained and reconstruction error;
  • explain why "maximise variance" and "minimise reconstruction error" are the same.

Words you need

Words you need
  • Unsupervised learning - finding structure in data that has no labels.
  • Representation learning - turning raw data into a better set of numbers (features) for later use.
  • Data matrix XXX - here d×nd\times nd×n: each column is one data point with ddd features.
  • Centring - subtracting the mean so the cloud is centred at the origin.
  • Covariance matrix CCC - how the features vary and move together.
  • Principal component (PC) - a unit direction of high variance; PC1 has the most, PC2 the next, and PC2 is perpendicular to PC1.
  • Reconstruction - rebuilding a data point from its few PCA coordinates.

Idea 1: The setup

You have nnn data points x1,…,xn∈Rd\mathbf x_1,\dots,\mathbf x_n\in\mathbb R^dx1​,…,xn​∈Rd. Stack them as the columns of X∈Rd×nX\in\mathbb R^{d\times n}X∈Rd×n.

Step 1: Centre. Compute the mean xˉ=1n∑xi\bar{\mathbf x}=\frac1n\sum\mathbf x_ixˉ=n1​∑xi​ and replace every point by xi−xˉ\mathbf x_i-\bar{\mathbf x}xi​−xˉ. From now on the data has mean zero.

Step 2: Covariance.

C=1n∑i=1nxixiT=1nXXT(d×d,  symmetric,  PSD)C=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\mathbf x_i\mathbf x_i^T=\frac1nXX^T\qquad(d\times d,\;\text{symmetric},\;\text{PSD})C=n1​i=1∑n​xi​xiT​=n1​XXT(d×d,symmetric,PSD)

(If your paper stores points as rows, the same matrix is 1nXTX\frac1nX^TXn1​XTX. Read the convention in the question.)

Step 3: The question PCA answers. Project onto a unit direction w\mathbf ww: each point becomes the number wTxi\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_iwTxi​. The spread (variance) of those numbers is

1n∑i(wTxi)2=wTCw\frac1n\sum_i(\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_i)^2=\mathbf w^TC\mathbf wn1​i∑​(wTxi​)2=wTCw

Which unit w\mathbf ww makes this largest? Maximise wTCw\mathbf w^TC\mathbf wwTCw subject to wTw=1\mathbf w^T\mathbf w=1wTw=1. The Lagrange method (MLF week 9) gives Cw=λwC\mathbf w=\lambda\mathbf wCw=λw: w\mathbf ww is an eigenvector of CCC, and the variance achieved is its eigenvalue λ\lambdaλ. So the best direction is the eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue. That is PC1. The next best direction perpendicular to PC1 is the eigenvector with the second largest eigenvalue, and so on.

The idea

The PCA recipe

  1. Centre the data.
  2. Form C=1nXXTC=\frac1nXX^TC=n1​XXT.
  3. Find its eigenvalues λ1≥λ2≥⋯≥λd≥0\lambda_1\ge\lambda_2\ge\dots\ge\lambda_d\ge0λ1​≥λ2​≥⋯≥λd​≥0 and unit eigenvectors w1,…,wd\mathbf w_1,\dots,\mathbf w_dw1​,…,wd​.
  4. Keep the top kkk eigenvectors: W=[w1⋯wk]W=[\mathbf w_1\cdots\mathbf w_k]W=[w1​⋯wk​].
  5. New coordinates of a point: z=WTx\mathbf z=W^T\mathbf xz=WTx (kkk numbers). Reconstruction: x^=Wz=WWTx\hat{\mathbf x}=W\mathbf z=WW^T\mathbf xx^=Wz=WWTx.

Idea 2: How good is the squashed version?

Variance explained. The total variance is λ1+⋯+λd=trace⁡(C)\lambda_1+\dots+\lambda_d=\operatorname{trace}(C)λ1​+⋯+λd​=trace(C). Keeping kkk components retains

λ1+⋯+λkλ1+⋯+λd\frac{\lambda_1+\dots+\lambda_k}{\lambda_1+\dots+\lambda_d}λ1​+⋯+λd​λ1​+⋯+λk​​

of it. People pick the smallest kkk that keeps, say, 90% or 95%.

Reconstruction error. The average squared distance between points and their reconstructions is

1n∑i∥xi−x^i∥2=λk+1+⋯+λd(the discarded eigenvalues)\frac1n\sum_i\|\mathbf x_i-\hat{\mathbf x}_i\|^2=\lambda_{k+1}+\dots+\lambda_d\qquad(\text{the discarded eigenvalues})n1​i∑​∥xi​−x^i​∥2=λk+1​+⋯+λd​(the discarded eigenvalues)
Think of it like this

Why are "most spread out" and "least squashing loss" the same question? Each point has a length from the centre. That length splits into the shadow on your axis and the leftover sticking out sideways, and Pythagoras says length2=shadow2+leftover2\text{length}^2=\text{shadow}^2+\text{leftover}^2length2=shadow2+leftover2. The total length of the cloud is fixed. So whatever you gain in shadow you lose in leftover. Maximising shadow-spread is the same as minimising leftover.

Idea 3: Practical notes and limits

  • Centre first. Without it the "first component" just points at the average and the covariance formula is wrong.
  • Scale matters. PCA chases variance, so a feature measured in thousands drowns out one measured in fractions. Standardise (divide each feature by its standard deviation) when units differ.
  • Components are uncorrelated. The new coordinates z1,z2,…z_1,z_2,\dotsz1​,z2​,… have zero covariance with each other, and Var⁡(zj)=λj\operatorname{Var}(z_j)=\lambda_jVar(zj​)=λj​.
  • At most min⁡(d,n−1)\min(d,n-1)min(d,n−1) useful components (centred data of nnn points has rank at most n−1n-1n−1).
  • PCA is linear: it can only find straight directions. A curved structure (like two concentric rings) fools it. The fix is kernel PCA (next week).
  • It is unsupervised: it never looks at labels, so the directions it keeps need not help classification.
  • PCA is not regression. Regression minimises vertical errors to predict yyy. PCA minimises perpendicular distances and treats every feature alike.

Link to SVD (MLF week 6). If X=UΣVTX=U\Sigma V^TX=UΣVT is the SVD of the centred d×nd\times nd×n matrix, the principal directions are the columns of UUU and λj=σj2/n\lambda_j=\sigma_j^2/nλj​=σj2​/n.

Exam radar

Exam radar

In MLT Quiz 1, the concept summaries mention PCA in 25 of 60 papers, covariance matrix in 18, PCA variance in 11, principal components in 10, variance explained in 9, reconstruction error in 9. PCA also appears in 14 End Term papers (with kernel PCA).

Typical items: compute a 2×22\times22×2 covariance from a few points and find its eigenvalues; variance explained by kkk components; reconstruct a point from PC1; "which statements about PCA are true"; the data is not centred, what changes?

Watch out - real exam trap
  • Reconstruction error = discarded eigenvalues, not the kept ones.
  • Eigenvectors for PCA must be unit length before you project.
  • Forgetting to centre is the most common blunder.
  • "PCA minimises the sum of vertical distances" is false: it is perpendicular distances.
  • Check whether the paper's CCC uses 1n\frac1nn1​ or 1n−1\frac1{n-1}n−11​; the question will say.

Recap

Remember this
  • PCA finds the directions of maximum variance: the eigenvectors of C=1nXXTC=\frac1nXX^TC=n1​XXT (after centring), ordered by eigenvalue.
  • Coordinates z=WTx\mathbf z=W^T\mathbf xz=WTx; reconstruction WWTxWW^T\mathbf xWWTx.
  • Variance explained =∑≤kλ/∑λ=\sum_{\le k}\lambda/\sum\lambda=∑≤k​λ/∑λ; reconstruction error =∑>kλ=\sum_{>k}\lambda=∑>k​λ.
  • Max variance ≡\equiv≡ min reconstruction error (Pythagoras). PCA is linear and unsupervised.
