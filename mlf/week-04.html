Week 4 · The special directions of a matrix

Official syllabus line: Linear Algebra - Eigenvalues and eigenvectors
About 80 minQuiz 1 material (see exam radar)BSCS2004 · Harish Guruprasad Ramaswamy, Arun Rajkumar, Prashanth LA
Before you start: Week 3 (matrices as machines, rank). Solving a quadratic equation x2+bx+c=0x^2+bx+c=0x2+bx+c=0.
🎬
Watch first: the animated story of this weekAbout 1-2 minutes of real 3D animation with a friendly voice. Pause any time.
Read the words of the video
  1. Imagine stretching a rubber sheet with arrows drawn on it. Most arrows twist and turn, pointing to new places after the stretch.
  2. But a few special arrows only get longer or shorter, still pointing exactly the same way. These are called eigenvectors.
  3. How much each special arrow grows or shrinks along its path is its eigenvalue. This number tells us the exact stretch factor.
  4. Mathematically, applying a matrix 'A' to an eigenvector 'v' just scales 'v' by a number, lambda. The direction stays the same!
  5. To find these special stretch numbers, we solve an equation: the determinant of the matrix minus lambda times the identity matrix must be zero.
  6. For a two-by-two matrix, there's a neat shortcut! The sum of the eigenvalues is the trace, and their product equals the determinant.
  7. For example, if the sum of two numbers is seven and their product is ten, the numbers are easily found as two and five by a simple quadratic equation.
  8. If you know a matrix's eigenvalues, you can quickly find eigenvalues for its inverse, powers, or when a constant is added.
  9. If a matrix has a zero eigenvalue, it squashes space flat and cannot be reversed or 'un-squashed.' It is not invertible.
  10. When we have enough independent eigenvectors, we can rewrite the matrix in a special way to easily calculate high powers, saving huge effort.
  11. Beware of common traps! Inverse eigenvalues are reciprocals, not negatives. Also, a repeated eigenvalue does not always mean a matrix cannot be diagonalized.
  12. So, eigenvectors show special directions, and eigenvalues show how much things stretch. Together, they simplify complex matrix operations and transformations.

The one-minute story

Think of it like this

Take a rubber sheet with a grid drawn on it and stretch it: pull it wider in one direction, squeeze it in another, maybe shear it. Now draw many arrows on the sheet before the stretch.

After the stretch, almost every arrow has been turned to point somewhere new. But a few special arrows only get longer or shorter, still pointing exactly the way they did (or exactly backwards). Those lucky arrows are the eigenvectors of the stretch. By how much each one grew or shrank is its eigenvalue.

Why care? Because along its special directions a complicated matrix behaves like plain multiplication by a number. Learn the special directions and the matrix stops being scary. Everything in PCA (MLF week 6, MLT week 1) is "find the special directions of the data".

By the end of this week you can:

  • say what an eigenvalue and eigenvector are, and find them for 2×22\times22×2 matrices;
  • use the trace and determinant shortcuts to get eigenvalues quickly;
  • find eigenvalues of A−1A^{-1}A−1, AkA^kAk, A+cIA+cIA+cI and ATA^TAT without recomputing;
  • decide when a matrix is diagonalisable and use A=PDP−1A=PDP^{-1}A=PDP−1 to compute powers.

Words you need

Words you need
  • Eigenvector v\mathbf vv - a non-zero vector whose direction AAA does not change.
  • Eigenvalue λ\lambdaλ - the stretch factor of that eigenvector: Av=λvA\mathbf v=\lambda\mathbf vAv=λv.
  • Characteristic equation - det⁡(A−λI)=0\det(A-\lambda I)=0det(A−λI)=0; its roots are the eigenvalues.
  • Trace - the sum of the diagonal entries of a matrix.
  • Determinant - a single number that says how a matrix scales area (or volume); zero means it squashes space flat.
  • Diagonal matrix - zeros everywhere except the diagonal. Easy to multiply and raise to powers.
  • Diagonalisable - can be rewritten as PDP−1PDP^{-1}PDP−1 with DDD diagonal.

Idea 1: The definition

Av=λv,v≠0A\mathbf v=\lambda\mathbf v,\qquad\mathbf v\neq\mathbf 0Av=λv,v=0

Read it aloud: "AAA applied to v\mathbf vv gives the same arrow, just scaled by λ\lambdaλ." We insist v≠0\mathbf v\ne\mathbf 0v=0 because the zero vector satisfies this for every λ\lambdaλ and tells us nothing.

  • λ>1\lambda>1λ>1: stretched. 0<λ<10<\lambda<10<λ<1: shrunk. λ<0\lambda<0λ<0: flipped to point the opposite way. λ=0\lambda=0λ=0: squashed to nothing.
Try it yourself

This matrix is (4123)\begin{pmatrix}4&1\\2&3\end{pmatrix}(42​13​). Spin the arrow v\mathbf vv and watch AvA\mathbf vAv. Use the buttons to find the two special directions.

How to find them

Rewrite Av=λvA\mathbf v=\lambda\mathbf vAv=λv as (A−λI)v=0(A-\lambda I)\mathbf v=\mathbf 0(A−λI)v=0. A non-zero v\mathbf vv can only be sent to zero if A−λIA-\lambda IA−λI squashes space flat, i.e. its determinant is zero:

det⁡(A−λI)=0\det(A-\lambda I)=0det(A−λI)=0

For a 2×22\times22×2 matrix this becomes a short quadratic with two handy facts:

λ2−(trace⁡A) λ+det⁡A=0\lambda^2-(\operatorname{trace}A)\,\lambda+\det A=0λ2−(traceA)λ+detA=0
The idea

The two free facts (true for every square matrix):

  • sum of eigenvalues === trace of AAA
  • product of eigenvalues === determinant of AAA

Use them to find a missing eigenvalue in seconds, or to sanity-check yours.

Solved example: 1. Eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a 2×22\times22×2

Question. Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of A=(4123)A=\begin{pmatrix}4&1\\2&3\end{pmatrix}A=(42​13​).

Solution.

  1. Trace =4+3=7=4+3=7=4+3=7, determinant =4⋅3−1⋅2=10=4\cdot3-1\cdot2=10=4⋅3−1⋅2=10. The equation is λ2−7λ+10=0\lambda^2-7\lambda+10=0λ2−7λ+10=0, which factorises as (λ−5)(λ−2)=0(\lambda-5)(\lambda-2)=0(λ−5)(λ−2)=0. So λ1=5\lambda_1=5λ1​=5, λ2=2\lambda_2=2λ2​=2.
  2. For λ=5\lambda=5λ=5: A−5I=(−112−2)A-5I=\begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\2&-2\end{pmatrix}A−5I=(−12​1−2​). Solving (A−5I)v=0(A-5I)\mathbf v=0(A−5I)v=0: −v1+v2=0-v_1+v_2=0−v1​+v2​=0, so v2=v1v_2=v_1v2​=v1​. Take v1=(1,1)\mathbf v_1=(1,1)v1​=(1,1).
  3. For λ=2\lambda=2λ=2: A−2I=(2121)A-2I=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\2&1\end{pmatrix}A−2I=(22​11​). Then 2v1+v2=02v_1+v_2=02v1​+v2​=0, so v2=−2v1v_2=-2v_1v2​=−2v1​. Take v2=(1,−2)\mathbf v_2=(1,-2)v2​=(1,−2).
  4. Check: A(1,1)=(5,5)=5(1,1)A(1,1)=(5,5)=5(1,1)A(1,1)=(5,5)=5(1,1) and A(1,−2)=(2,−4)=2(1,−2)A(1,-2)=(2,-4)=2(1,-2)A(1,−2)=(2,−4)=2(1,−2).

Any non-zero multiple of an eigenvector is also an eigenvector, so (2,2)(2,2)(2,2) works just as well as (1,1)(1,1)(1,1).

Idea 3: When eigenvalues are complex, repeated, or not enough

Complex eigenvalues. A rotation by 90∘90^\circ90∘, (0−110)\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}(01​−10​), turns every arrow, so no real arrow stays on its line. Its characteristic equation λ2+1=0\lambda^2+1=0λ2+1=0 gives λ=±i\lambda=\pm iλ=±i. Real matrices can have complex eigenvalues; real symmetric matrices cannot (week 5).

Repeated eigenvalues. The number of times λ\lambdaλ appears as a root is its algebraic multiplicity. The number of independent eigenvectors it has is its geometric multiplicity, which is never bigger.

The idea

Diagonalisable means you can find enough eigenvectors: nnn independent ones for an n×nn\times nn×n matrix. Then, with P=[v1  ⋯  vn]P=[\mathbf v_1\;\cdots\;\mathbf v_n]P=[v1​⋯vn​] and D=diag⁡(λ1,…,λn)D=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)D=diag(λ1​,…,λn​):

A=PDP−1⟹Ak=PDkP−1A=PDP^{-1}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad A^k=PD^kP^{-1}A=PDP−1⟹Ak=PDkP−1

Eigenvectors for different eigenvalues are always independent. Trouble only arises with repeated eigenvalues.

Watch out - real exam trap

"The eigenvalue is repeated, so it is not diagonalisable" is wrong. So is "...so it is diagonalisable".

  • The identity matrix III has eigenvalue 1 twice and is already diagonal. Diagonalisable.
  • The shear (1101)\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}(10​11​) has eigenvalue 1 twice, but A−I=(0100)A-I=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}A−I=(00​10​) only has one independent eigenvector (1,0)(1,0)(1,0). Not diagonalisable.

A repeated eigenvalue by itself tells you nothing about diagonalisability. Check how many independent eigenvectors it has.

Solved example: 4. A matrix that is not diagonalisable

Question. Is B=(2102)B=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}B=(20​12​) diagonalisable?

Solution. It is triangular, so the eigenvalues are the diagonal entries: 222 and 222 (repeated). Solve (B−2I)v=0(B-2I)\mathbf v=0(B−2I)v=0: B−2I=(0100)B-2I=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}B−2I=(00​10​) gives v2=0v_2=0v2​=0, so the only eigenvectors are multiples of (1,0)(1,0)(1,0). Watch out - real exam trap
  • Eigenvalues of A−1A^{-1}A−1 are reciprocals, not negatives.
  • Eigenvalues of A+cIA+cIA+cI shift by ccc; they do not multiply by anything.
  • Product of eigenvalues is the determinant, sum is the trace. Do not swap.
  • AAA and ATA^TAT share eigenvalues, but not necessarily eigenvectors.
  • "Repeated eigenvalue" does not decide diagonalisability.

Quick quiz

Quick quiz

A 2 by 2 matrix has trace 5 and determinant 6. Its eigenvalues are...

  • 1 and 6
  • 2 and 3
  • 5 and 6
  • 3 and 3
They are the roots of x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0, which are 2 and 3.

A has eigenvalues 2 and 5. The eigenvalues of A inverse are...

  • -2 and -5
  • 1/2 and 1/5
  • 2 and 5
  • 4 and 25
The eigenvalues of A inverse are reciprocals.

Which matrix is NOT diagonalisable?

  • the identity matrix
  • [[1, 1], [0, 1]]
  • [[2, 0], [0, 3]]
  • [[1, 2], [2, 1]]
The shear has the repeated eigenvalue 1 with only one independent eigenvector. The identity and the diagonal matrix are already diagonal; the symmetric matrix is always diagonalisable.

Select all that are TRUE. (select all that apply)

  • The sum of the eigenvalues equals the trace.
  • A matrix with a zero eigenvalue is not invertible.
  • Eigenvectors for different eigenvalues are always perpendicular.
  • A repeated eigenvalue means the matrix cannot be diagonalised.
Perpendicularity is only guaranteed for symmetric matrices. Repeated eigenvalues do not by themselves rule out diagonalisation.
