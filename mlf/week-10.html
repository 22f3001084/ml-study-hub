The one-minute story

Think of it like this

Toss a coin 100 times and you will see about 50 heads, but never exactly "head, tail, head, tail". The world is uncertain, and data is a messy, noisy sample of it. Probability is the language for talking about that uncertainty precisely, and machine learning is full of it: "the model says 83% spam", "the average error is about 2 give or take 0.3", "with 95% confidence the true value is in this range".

This week is a tool kit:

  1. the rules of probability (and Bayes' rule, which lets you flip "what I saw" into "what is probably true");
  2. random variables, their average (expectation) and spread (variance);
  3. the handful of distributions you will meet again and again;
  4. four big results that say why averaging data works: Markov, Chebyshev, the law of large numbers, the central limit theorem.

By the end of this week you can:

  • use probability rules, conditional probability, independence and Bayes' rule;
  • compute expectation and variance, and use joint and marginal densities;
  • work with Bernoulli, binomial, uniform, exponential and normal distributions;
  • bound probabilities with Markov and Chebyshev, and explain the law of large numbers and central limit theorem.

  • Probability P(A)P(A)P(A) - a number from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain).
  • Random variable XXX - a number whose value is decided by chance.
  • Expectation E[X]E[X]E[X] - the long-run average value.
  • Variance Var⁡(X)\operatorname{Var}(X)Var(X) - how spread out XXX is around its average. Standard deviation σ=Var⁡\sigma=\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}}σ=Var​.
  • Density f(x)f(x)f(x) - for a continuous variable, the height of the curve whose area gives probability.

Idea 1: The rules of probability

The idea
  • 0≤P(A)≤10\le P(A)\le10≤P(A)≤1, and P(everything)=1P(\text{everything})=1P(everything)=1.
  • Complement: P(not A)=1−P(A)P(\text{not }A)=1-P(A)P(not A)=1−P(A).
  • Either-or: P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A∩B)P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A∩B).
  • Conditional: P(A∣B)=P(A∩B)P(B)P(A\mid B)=\dfrac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}P(A∣B)=P(B)P(A∩B)​, "the chance of AAA once we know BBB happened".
  • Independent events: P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B)P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B).
  • Bayes' rule: P(A∣B)=P(B∣A) P(A)P(B)P(A\mid B)=\dfrac{P(B\mid A)\,P(A)}{P(B)}P(A∣B)=P(B)P(B∣A)P(A)​.
Think of it like this

Bayes' rule flips a question around. A doctor knows "if a person has the disease, the test is positive 90% of the time". But you want to know the opposite: "my test is positive, so how likely is it that I really have the disease?" Bayes' rule converts one into the other, using how common the disease is to begin with.

Solved example: 1. Bayes' rule on a medical test

Question. 1% of people have a disease. A test is positive for 90% of sick people but also for 5% of healthy people. You test positive. What is the chance you are sick?

Solution.

  1. P(D)=0.01P(D)=0.01P(D)=0.01, P(+∣D)=0.9P(+\mid D)=0.9P(+∣D)=0.9, P(+∣not D)=0.05P(+\mid\text{not }D)=0.05P(+∣not D)=0.05.
  2. P(+)=P(+∣D)P(D)+P(+∣not D)P(not D)=0.9(0.01)+0.05(0.99)=0.009+0.0495=0.0585P(+)=P(+\mid D)P(D)+P(+\mid\text{not }D)P(\text{not }D)=0.9(0.01)+0.05(0.99)=0.009+0.0495=0.0585P(+)=P(+∣D)P(D)+P(+∣not D)P(not D)=0.9(0.01)+0.05(0.99)=0.009+0.0495=0.0585.
  3. P(D∣+)=0.9×0.010.0585≈0.154P(D\mid+)=\dfrac{0.9\times0.01}{0.0585}\approx\mathbf{0.154}P(D∣+)=0.05850.9×0.01​≈0.154.

Only about 15%, because the disease is rare, so most positives are false alarms from the huge healthy group. This idea (a prior belief updated by evidence) returns in MLT's Bayesian estimation and Naive Bayes.

Question. A fair coin is tossed twice. AAA: first toss is heads. BBB: second toss is heads. CCC: both tosses are the same. Are A,B,CA,B,CA,B,C independent?

Solution. P(A)=P(B)=P(C)=12P(A)=P(B)=P(C)=\frac12P(A)=P(B)=P(C)=21​ (CCC = HH or TT).

  • P(A∩C)=P(HH)=14=P(A)P(C)P(A\cap C)=P(HH)=\frac14=P(A)P(C)P(A∩C)=P(HH)=41​=P(A)P(C). ✓

Idea 2: Random variables, expectation and variance

A discrete random variable takes separate values with a probability mass function p(x)=P(X=x)p(x)=P(X=x)p(x)=P(X=x). A continuous one has a density f(x)f(x)f(x), and P(a≤X≤b)=∫abf(x) dxP(a\le X\le b)=\int_a^bf(x)\,dxP(a≤X≤b)=∫ab​f(x)dx (area under the curve). The total area is 1.

E[X]=∑xx p(x)or∫xf(x) dx,Var⁡(X)=E[X2]−(E[X])2E[X]=\sum_x x\,p(x)\quad\text{or}\quad\int x f(x)\,dx,\qquad\operatorname{Var}(X)=E[X^2]-(E[X])^2E[X]=x∑​xp(x)or∫xf(x)dx,Var(X)=E[X2]−(E[X])2
The idea

Idea 4: Four results that explain why data works

Markov and Chebyshev: bounds with almost no information

The idea

Markov (for X≥0X\ge0X≥0, knowing only the mean): for any a>0a>0a>0

P(X≥a)≤E[X]aP(X\ge a)\le\frac{E[X]}{a}P(X≥a)≤aE[X]​

Chebyshev (knowing mean μ\muμ and variance σ2\sigma^2σ2): for any k>0k>0k>0

P(∣X−μ∣≥kσ)≤1k2,equivalentlyP(∣X−μ∣≥ε)≤σ2ε2P(|X-\mu|\ge k\sigma)\le\frac1{k^2},\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad P(|X-\mu|\ge\varepsilon)\le\frac{\sigma^2}{\varepsilon^2}P(∣X−μ∣≥kσ)≤k21​,equivalentlyP(∣X−μ∣≥ε)≤ε2σ2​
Think of it like this

Markov: "The average person in the class is 12 years old, so at most one-sixth of them can be 72 or older." There just isn't enough "age" to go round. Chebyshev: "If most people are close to the average, few can be very far." Both give guarantees that hold for any distribution, which is why they are conservative.

The law of large numbers and the central limit theorem

Take nnn independent repeats X1,…,XnX_1,\dots,X_nX1​,…,Xn​ of the same experiment with mean μ\muμ and variance σ2\sigma^2σ2, and average them: Xˉn=1n∑Xi\bar X_n=\frac1n\sum X_iXˉn​=n1​∑Xi​. Then E[Xˉn]=μE[\bar X_n]=\muE[Xˉn​]=μ and Var⁡(Xˉn)=σ2/n\operatorname{Var}(\bar X_n)=\sigma^2/nVar(Xˉn​)=σ2/n.

  • Weak Law of Large Numbers (WLLN): as nnn grows, Xˉn\bar X_nXˉn​ gets close to μ\muμ. Chebyshev makes it precise: P(∣Xˉn−μ∣≥ε)≤σ2nε2→0P(|\bar X_n-\mu|\ge\varepsilon)\le\dfrac{\sigma^2}{n\varepsilon^2}\to0P(∣Xˉn​−μ∣≥ε)≤nε2σ2​→0.
  • Central Limit Theorem (CLT): for large nnn, Xˉn≈N(μ,σ2/n)\bar X_n\approx N(\mu,\sigma^2/n)Xˉn​≈N(μ,σ2/n), whatever shape the original distribution had. This is why bell curves show up everywhere.

Watch out - real exam trap
  • Markov needs a non-negative variable. Chebyshev needs mean and variance, and its bound is for the two-sided event ∣X−μ∣≥kσ|X-\mu|\ge k\sigma∣X−μ∣≥kσ.
  • Chebyshev's σ\sigmaσ is the standard deviation, not the variance; the sample-mean version uses σ2/n\sigma^2/nσ2/n.
  • Pairwise independence ≠\ne= mutual independence.
  • Covariance 0≠0\ne0= independent.
  • For Var⁡(aX)\operatorname{Var}(aX)Var(aX) remember a2a^2a2. For independent sums, variances add; standard deviations do not.
  • Exponential: mean is 1/λ1/\lambda1/λ, not λ\lambdaλ.

  • Chebyshev's inequality needs the variable to be non-negative.
Zero covariance does not imply independence. Chebyshev needs only a mean and variance.
