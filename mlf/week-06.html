Read the words of the video
  1. Imagine kneading dough. You turn it, stretch it long in one direction, then turn it again. This simple three-step recipe is exactly how complex data transformations work! We call this amazing process Singular Value Decomposition, or SVD. It helps us understand how any matrix changes space.
  2. SVD helps us find the most important 'stretches' in data. Like a photo: most information is in the biggest stretches. Discarding smaller stretches makes a smaller file, still looking great! PCA uses this for data points: finding the view that shows maximum spread, using SVD's powerful idea.
  3. Let’s look at three data points like beads on a string. First, we center them around the middle. Then, using PCA, we find the best line through them. This line shows 100 percent of their spread, meaning all their movement is perfectly captured by just one direction!

The one-minute story

Think of it like this

Knead a ball of dough: you turn it, stretch it along one direction, then turn it again. Any matrix, however messy, acts on space exactly like that: a turn, a stretch along the coordinate axes, and another turn. This three-step recipe is the singular value decomposition (SVD). The stretch amounts are called singular values, and the big ones tell you where the "action" of the matrix lives.

Now think of a photograph, which is just a big table of brightness numbers (a matrix). Most of its information sits in the first few stretch amounts; the rest is fine detail you would hardly miss. Throw the small ones away and you get a smaller file that still looks almost identical. That is image compression.

PCA (principal component analysis) uses the same idea on a cloud of data points: "Which flat view of this cloud shows the most spread?" You will find the answer is the SVD (or the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix) of the data.

By the end of this week you can:

  • state the SVD A=UΣVTA=U\Sigma V^TA=UΣVT and compute singular values from ATAA^TAATA;
  • connect rank with the number of non-zero singular values;
  • build the best low-rank approximation and count how much storage it saves;
  • compute a covariance matrix and run PCA by hand on small data;
  • compute variance explained and reconstruction error.

Words you need

Words you need
  • Singular values σ1≥σ2≥⋯≥0\sigma_1\ge\sigma_2\ge\dots\ge0σ1​≥σ2​≥⋯≥0 - the stretch amounts of the SVD. Never negative.
  • Singular vectors - the columns of VVV (input directions) and UUU (output directions).
  • Low-rank approximation - replacing a matrix by a simpler one that uses only its biggest singular values.
  • Centering - subtracting the average from each feature so the data cloud is centred at the origin.
  • Covariance matrix - a table of how every pair of features moves together; the diagonal holds the variances.
  • Principal component - a direction along which the data is spread out the most.
  • Variance explained - the share of the total spread captured by the chosen directions.

Idea 1: The singular value decomposition

The idea

Every matrix AAA (any shape, any rank) can be written

A=U Σ VTA=U\,\Sigma\,V^TA=UΣVT
  • VVV is an n×nn\times nn×n orthogonal matrix (the first turn),
  • Σ\SigmaΣ is an m×nm\times nm×n matrix that is zero except for the non-negative numbers σ1≥σ2≥…\sigma_1\ge\sigma_2\ge\dotsσ1​≥σ2​≥… on its diagonal (the stretch),
  • UUU is an m×mm\times mm×m orthogonal matrix (the final turn).

How to get them:

  • singular values σi=eigenvalues of ATA\sigma_i=\sqrt{\text{eigenvalues of }A^TA}σi​=eigenvalues of ATA​;
  • right singular vectors (columns of VVV) are the eigenvectors of ATAA^TAATA;
  • left singular vectors (columns of UUU) are the eigenvectors of AATAA^TAAT, with ui=Avi/σi\mathbf u_i=A\mathbf v_i/\sigma_iui​=Avi​/σi​.

ATAA^TAATA is symmetric and positive semi-definite (week 5), so its eigenvalues are real and ≥0\ge0≥0, which is why the square roots make sense.

Rank: the rank of AAA equals the number of non-zero singular values.

Writing it out term by term:

A=σ1u1v1T+σ2u2v2T+⋯+σrurvrTA=\sigma_1\mathbf u_1\mathbf v_1^T+\sigma_2\mathbf u_2\mathbf v_2^T+\cdots+\sigma_r\mathbf u_r\mathbf v_r^TA=σ1​u1​v1T​+σ2​u2​v2T​+⋯+σr​ur​vrT​

Each term is a rank-1 piece, and they are listed from biggest to smallest.

Watch out - real exam trap

Singular values are not eigenvalues. For B=(300−2)B=\begin{pmatrix}3&0\\0&-2\end{pmatrix}B=(30​0−2​) the eigenvalues are 333 and −2-2−2, but the singular values are 333 and 222, because singular values can never be negative and come from BTB=diag⁡(9,4)B^TB=\operatorname{diag}(9,4)BTB=diag(9,4). They coincide with eigenvalues only for symmetric positive semi-definite matrices. Exam options love "singular values are the eigenvalues of AAA".

Facts that appear as true/false statements

  • SVD exists for every matrix, square or not.
  • σi≥0\sigma_i\ge0σi​≥0 always. The largest, σ1\sigma_1σ1​, is the biggest amount AAA can stretch a unit vector: ∥A∥2=σ1\|A\|_2=\sigma_1∥A∥2​=σ1​.
  • AAA is m×nm\times nm×n of rank rrr ⇒\Rightarrow⇒ exactly rrr non-zero singular values (and min⁡(m,n)−r\min(m,n)-rmin(m,n)−r zero ones).
  • A 10×810\times810×8 matrix of rank 888 has 888 non-zero singular values, so ATAA^TAATA (8×88\times88×8) is invertible. That is exactly the condition for a unique least-squares solution (week 3).

Low-rank approximation and image compression

Eckart-Young theorem: among all matrices of rank kkk, the closest one to AAA is obtained by keeping only the first kkk terms of the sum above. The error is made only of the discarded singular values: ∥A−Ak∥F2=σk+12+σk+22+⋯\|A-A_k\|_F^2=\sigma_{k+1}^2+\sigma_{k+2}^2+\cdots∥A−Ak​∥F2​=σk+12​+σk+22​+⋯.

Solved example: 2. How much does compression save?

Question. A grey-scale image is a 100×100100\times100100×100 matrix. You keep only its top 555 singular values. How many numbers must you store, compared with the original?

Solution. The original needs 100×100=10,000100\times100=10{,}000100×100=10,000 numbers. The rank-5 version ∑i=15σiuiviT\sum_{i=1}^5\sigma_i\mathbf u_i\mathbf v_i^T∑i=15​σi​ui​viT​ needs, for each iii, one σi\sigma_iσi​, one 100100100-long ui\mathbf u_iui​ and one 100100100-long vi\mathbf v_ivi​: 5×(100+100+1)=10055\times(100+100+1)=10055×(100+100+1)=1005 numbers. About 10 % of the storage, and for a typical photo the picture is still recognisable.

Idea 2: PCA, finding the best view of a data cloud

Say you have nnn data points, each with ddd features, stored as the columns of a d×nd\times nd×n matrix XXX.

Step 1: Centre. Subtract the mean of each feature, so the cloud is centred at the origin. (Skip this and PCA is wrong: the first "component" would just point at the average.)

Step 2: Covariance matrix.

C=1n∑i=1nxixiT=1nXXTC=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\mathbf x_i\mathbf x_i^T=\frac1nXX^TC=n1​i=1∑n​xi​xiT​=n1​XXT

The diagonal entries CjjC_{jj}Cjj​ are the variances of each feature, and CjkC_{jk}Cjk​ measures how features jjj and kkk rise and fall together. CCC is symmetric and positive semi-definite.

Step 3: Eigen-analysis. The principal components are the eigenvectors of CCC, ordered by eigenvalue. The eigenvalue λk\lambda_kλk​ is the variance of the data along component kkk.

Why? The spread of the data along a unit direction u\mathbf uu is 1n∑(uTxi)2=uTCu\frac1n\sum(\mathbf u^T\mathbf x_i)^2=\mathbf u^TC\mathbf un1​∑(uTxi​)2=uTCu. We want the u\mathbf uu with ∥u∥=1\|\mathbf u\|=1∥u∥=1 that makes this as big as possible. Week 9's Lagrange multipliers will show the answer satisfies Cu=λuC\mathbf u=\lambda\mathbf uCu=λu and the value reached is λ\lambdaλ. The biggest spread is the top eigenvalue and its eigenvector.

Try it yourself

The black dots are your data, already centred. Turn the axis and watch the spread of the orange shadows. The red sticks show what you lose by squashing onto the axis.

Two ways to say what PCA does

  • Maximise the spread (variance) of the shadows on the chosen axes.
  • Minimise the reconstruction error: the squared distance between each point and its shadow.

Since each point satisfies ∥x∥2=∥shadow∥2+∥leftover∥2\|\mathbf x\|^2=\|\text{shadow}\|^2+\|\text{leftover}\|^2∥x∥2=∥shadow∥2+∥leftover∥2 (Pythagoras), these are the same problem. The animation shows it: the total never changes, so as shadow-spread rises, the loss falls.

The idea

With eigenvalues λ1≥λ2≥⋯≥λd\lambda_1\ge\lambda_2\ge\dots\ge\lambda_dλ1​≥λ2​≥⋯≥λd​ of CCC:

  • variance explained by the top kkk components =λ1+⋯+λkλ1+⋯+λd=\dfrac{\lambda_1+\dots+\lambda_k}{\lambda_1+\dots+\lambda_d}=λ1​+⋯+λd​λ1​+⋯+λk​​
  • reconstruction error (average squared) when keeping kkk components =λk+1+⋯+λd=\lambda_{k+1}+\dots+\lambda_d=λk+1​+⋯+λd​ (the discarded eigenvalues)
  • projecting x\mathbf xx onto the top kkk components W=[w1⋯wk]W=[\mathbf w_1\cdots\mathbf w_k]W=[w1​⋯wk​]: coordinates WTxW^T\mathbf xWTx, reconstruction WWTxWW^T\mathbf xWWTx (a projection matrix, week 3)

The link to SVD. If X=UΣVTX=U\Sigma V^TX=UΣVT is the SVD of the centred data matrix, then C=1nXXT=UΣ2nUTC=\frac1nXX^T=U\frac{\Sigma^2}{n}U^TC=n1​XXT=UnΣ2​UT. So the principal components are the left singular vectors ui\mathbf u_iui​, and λi=σi2/n\lambda_i=\sigma_i^2/nλi​=σi2​/n. PCA and SVD are two names for one computation.

True statements about PCA (the exam's favourite MSQ)

  • It is unsupervised (no labels used).
  • Components are orthogonal (eigenvectors of a symmetric matrix) and the projected features are uncorrelated.
  • Variance explained by a component is its eigenvalue divided by the sum of all eigenvalues.
  • You must centre the data; if features have different units you should also scale them.
  • With nnn points and ddd features there are at most min⁡(n,d)\min(n,d)min(n,d) useful components (centering removes one more: rank of the centred data is at most n−1n-1n−1).
  • PCA is not linear regression: regression measures vertical errors to predict yyy; PCA measures perpendicular distances and treats all features alike.

Watch out - real exam trap
  • Divide by nnn (or n−1n-1n−1) when forming CCC, according to what the question says. In this course papers use 1nXXT\frac1nXX^Tn1​XXT.
  • Reconstruction error = the sum of the discarded eigenvalues, not the kept ones.
  • Singular values ≠\ne= eigenvalues of AAA.
  • Forgetting to centre the data before computing CCC.
  • Principal components are eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, not of the raw data matrix.
