The one-minute story

Think of it like this

A detective arrives at a scene and finds clues. Many different stories could explain them, but one story makes the clues the least surprising. A good detective picks that story. That is maximum likelihood estimation (MLE): among all possible settings of the model, pick the one under which the data you actually saw was most likely.

Now make the detective's job harder. Two jars of sweets are on a table, and a friend, with eyes closed, picks a jar each time and hands you a handful, but never tells you which jar. You want each jar's colour mix, but the labels are hidden. What do you do?

You guess which jar each handful probably came from (using your current idea of the jars), then re-estimate the jars using those guesses, and repeat. Each round your guesses and your jar recipes improve together. That loop is the EM algorithm (Expectation-Maximisation), and it lets us fit models with hidden pieces.

By the end of this week you can:

  • write down a likelihood and find the MLE for common models by calculus;
  • explain the bias of the Gaussian variance MLE;
  • state the idea of MAP estimation;
  • explain latent variables and run one step of EM by hand on the two-coin problem.

Words you need

Words you need
  • Likelihood L(θ)L(\theta)L(θ) - the probability (or density) of the observed data, viewed as a function of the parameter θ\thetaθ.
  • Log-likelihood ℓ(θ)=ln⁡L(θ)\ell(\theta)=\ln L(\theta)ℓ(θ)=lnL(θ) - easier to work with; its peak is in the same place.
  • MLE θ^\hat\thetaθ^ - the parameter value that maximises the likelihood.
  • Estimator - a recipe that turns data into a guess for a parameter. It is unbiased if its guesses average to the truth.
  • Latent (hidden) variable - something the model needs but we never observe, like "which jar".
  • Responsibility - the current probability that a data point came from a given hidden cause.
  • Prior and posterior - what you believe about θ\thetaθ before and after seeing data.

Idea 1: Maximum likelihood estimation

Say x1,…,xnx_1,\dots,x_nx1​,…,xn​ are independent draws from a distribution with density or mass p(x∣θ)p(x\mid\theta)p(x∣θ). The probability of the whole data set is the product:

L(θ)=∏i=1np(xi∣θ)ℓ(θ)=∑i=1nln⁡p(xi∣θ)L(\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^np(x_i\mid\theta)\qquad\ell(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^n\ln p(x_i\mid\theta)L(θ)=i=1∏n​p(xi​∣θ)ℓ(θ)=i=1∑n​lnp(xi​∣θ)
The idea

The MLE recipe

  1. Write the likelihood L(θ)L(\theta)L(θ).
  2. Take the log (products become sums, and the maximiser does not move because ln⁡\lnln is increasing).
  3. Differentiate with respect to θ\thetaθ and set it to zero.
  4. Solve, and check it is a maximum.

Likelihood is not the probability that θ\thetaθ is true. It is how probable the data is if θ\thetaθ were true. We vary θ\thetaθ and keep the data fixed.

MLE and MAP

If you also have a prior belief p(θ)p(\theta)p(θ), the posterior is p(θ∣data)∝p(data∣θ) p(θ)p(\theta\mid\text{data})\propto p(\text{data}\mid\theta)\,p(\theta)p(θ∣data)∝p(data∣θ)p(θ), and the MAP (maximum a posteriori) estimate maximises it. MLE is MAP with a "flat" prior. With lots of data, the two agree. MAP gives you regularisation (ridge and LASSO in MLT week 6).

Link to least squares. If y=wTx+noisey=\mathbf w^T\mathbf x+\text{noise}y=wTx+noise with Gaussian noise, the log-likelihood is −12σ2∑(yi−wTxi)2+const-\frac1{2\sigma^2}\sum(y_i-\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_i)^2+\text{const}−2σ21​∑(yi​−wTxi​)2+const. Maximising it means minimising the squared error: so least squares is MLE under Gaussian noise (week 3 and MLT week 5).

Idea 2: When something is hidden

Fitting a mixture of distributions: a data point comes from one of KKK components, but we do not see which. The density is

p(x∣θ)=∑k=1Kπk pk(x∣θk)p(x\mid\theta)=\sum_{k=1}^K\pi_k\,p_k(x\mid\theta_k)p(x∣θ)=k=1∑K​πk​pk​(x∣θk​)

Now ℓ(θ)=∑iln⁡(∑kπkpk(xi∣θk))\ell(\theta)=\sum_i\ln\Big(\sum_k\pi_kp_k(x_i\mid\theta_k)\Big)ℓ(θ)=∑i​ln(∑k​πk​pk​(xi​∣θk​)) has a log of a sum, which does not simplify, and setting the derivative to zero gives no tidy formula. If we knew which component produced each point, MLE would be easy (just estimate each component from its own points). The EM algorithm exploits exactly this.

Idea 3: The EM algorithm

Start with a guess for the parameters, then repeat two steps:

The idea

E-step (Expectation): with the current parameters, compute for every data point the responsibility: the probability that it came from each hidden cause. These are soft, fractional guesses.

M-step (Maximisation): pretend those fractional guesses are real. Re-estimate every parameter by ordinary MLE, weighting each data point by its responsibility.

Facts to remember:

  • Each full round never decreases the likelihood, so EM climbs steadily.
  • It converges to a local maximum, not necessarily the global one, so the starting guess matters.
  • It is k-means' soft cousin: k-means assigns each point to one cluster (a hard guess); EM gives each point a fractional membership.

Your turn: 3. Unbiased or not?

Why do we sometimes divide by n−1n-1n−1 instead of nnn when estimating a variance?

Need a hint?

The sample mean is itself estimated from the same data.

Show the full solution

Data points sit slightly closer to their own sample mean than to the true mean, so 1n∑(xi−xˉ)2\frac1n\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2n1​∑(xi​−xˉ)2 underestimates the true variance on average. Multiplying by nn−1\frac n{n-1}n−1n​ corrects that, giving the unbiased estimator. The MLE (with nnn) is biased but consistent: the bias vanishes as nnn grows.

Your turn: 5. Run EM mentally

Why is the E-step in the two-coin problem needed at all? What would be easy if the coin labels were known?

Need a hint?

Think about what MLE would do per coin.

Show the full solution

If the labels were known, each coin's bias would just be (heads with that coin) / (tosses with that coin). The E-step fills in the missing labels with probabilities (responsibilities), and the M-step does exactly that same ratio, but with weighted counts.

Watch out - real exam trap
  • Maximise the log-likelihood but report the parameter, not the maximum value, unless asked.
  • Normal variance MLE uses 1n\frac1nn1​; the unbiased version uses 1n−1\frac1{n-1}n−11​. Read which one the question asks for.
  • For an exponential the MLE of the rate is 1/xˉ1/\bar x1/xˉ, not xˉ\bar xxˉ.
  • EM finds a local maximum and depends on the start.
  • Responsibilities across components must add to 1 for each data point.
